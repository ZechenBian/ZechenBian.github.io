:::lede
A large language model is the transformer of week 7, made larger and trained on far more text, then adapted to follow instructions. Most of what is new is not architecture but engineering and measurement: how text becomes tokens, what goes into the training data, and how the loss depends on the size of the model and of the data. That last dependence is regular enough to plan with. Start by spending a compute budget.

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Train one model of text that can then be steered to many tasks: predict the next token on a large corpus, then adapt the model by fine-tuning on examples or by prompting it.
**Why can it be done this way?** Next-token prediction needs no labels, so the training set can be most of the useful text there is (sections 3 and 4). Byte-pair encoding turns any text into a sequence from a fixed vocabulary of manageable length (section 2), and the decoder-only transformer trains on all positions of a sequence at once (section 1).
**Why should it be done this way?** Predicting text well requires modelling what the text is about (week 2: a better model is a better compressor), and the test loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute over many orders of magnitude, so the payoff of scale can be predicted before it is paid for (section 5).
**What comes out?** The GPT line from 117 million to 175 billion parameters, in-context learning, the Llama models trained on up to 15 trillion tokens (section 6), and a rule for splitting compute between parameters and data, about 20 tokens per parameter, derived in section 5. Two experiments: BPE learned on Shakespeare, and the compute-optimal split, including the counting that misled Kaplan et al.
:::

## 1. Decoder-only language models

The model is week 7's causal transformer: token and position embeddings, $L$ blocks of masked self-attention and MLP with residual connections and normalization, and a linear map back to the vocabulary. It defines $p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})$ for every position at once, and it is trained by minimizing the average cross-entropy over all positions of all training sequences:
$$\mathcal L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\sum_j T_j}\sum_j\sum_{t=1}^{T_j}\log p_\theta\bigl(x^{(j)}_t \bigm| x^{(j)}_{<t}\bigr).$$
By week 2 this is the code length of the training text under the model; by week 3 it is maximum likelihood. Nothing in it mentions any task.

*Decoder-only* means there is no separate encoder as in week 6's translation models: the input and the output are one sequence, and the model continues it. Encoder–decoder transformers (T5) and bidirectional encoders (BERT) were competitive for years; the decoder-only design won at scale, for reasons that are partly engineering (one stack, one objective, every token a training signal) and partly that generation subsumes the other uses.

:::thm Heuristic | Why next-token prediction teaches more than syntax
To predict the next word of a news article, a proof or a dialogue as well as possible, a model has to represent what the text refers to: facts, arithmetic, the goals of the characters. Lower loss on diverse text therefore forces richer internal representations, and capabilities that look unrelated to prediction appear as side effects. This is an argument from the objective, not a theorem: how much of a capability the loss rewards depends on how often the text exercises it.
:::

## 2. Tokenization: byte-pair encoding

A model's vocabulary is a design choice. With characters (or bytes), the vocabulary is tiny and nothing is unknown, but sequences are long: an English page is about 3,000 characters, and attention costs grow quadratically with length. With words, sequences are short, but the vocabulary is huge, rare words are seen too seldom to learn, and new words, names, numbers and typos are out of vocabulary. Subword tokenization interpolates.

:::thm Definition | Byte-pair encoding (Gage 1994; Sennrich, Haddow and Birch 2016)
*Learning.* Split the training text into words (GPT-2 keeps each word's leading space), and write each word as a sequence of base symbols, characters or bytes. Repeat $M$ times: count every adjacent pair of symbols, weighted by word frequency; take the most frequent pair $(a, b)$; replace every occurrence of $a\,b$ by a new symbol $ab$ and record the merge. *Encoding.* Split new text into words and apply the recorded merges to each word, in the order they were learned. The vocabulary is the base symbols plus the $M$ merged ones.
:::

:::fig bpefig | Encoding " lower" with merges learned on English: each step joins the adjacent pair that was most frequent in training, in the order learned, until no learned merge applies. Two tokens remain.

:::thm Proposition | What BPE does and does not optimize
1. Each merge greedily maximizes the reduction in the number of tokens on the training text: merging a pair with count $n$ removes exactly $n$ tokens (counting the pair's non-overlapping occurrences).
2. With bytes as base symbols, every string can be encoded, so nothing is ever out of vocabulary.
3. BPE does not in general find the vocabulary of a given size that minimizes the number of tokens; it is a greedy heuristic.
:::proof Proof
(1) Each non-overlapping occurrence of $a\,b$ becomes one symbol instead of two. (2) Every byte is in the base vocabulary, and merges only combine existing symbols. (3) Choosing a vocabulary of a given size that minimizes the number of tokens is NP-hard (Whittington, Bachmann and Pimentel 2024), and small examples in which an early greedy merge blocks two better ones are easy to build.
:::

GPT-2 used byte-level BPE with 50,257 symbols. Llama 3 uses about 128,000. For English text these tokenizers produce about one token per four characters; the first experiment gets to 2.4 characters per token with only 1,000 merges learned on Shakespeare. The cost of the choice is borne unevenly: Petrov et al. (2023) found the same sentence can take many times more tokens in some languages than in English, which makes those languages slower and more expensive to process and leaves less of the context window for them. Tokenization also explains some odd failures, such as miscounting the letters of a word the model only ever sees as one or two tokens.

## 3. Pretraining and fine-tuning

:::fig stages | How a chat model is made. Pretraining on next-token prediction uses almost all the compute and is where the knowledge comes from. The later stages use small datasets to shape behaviour: answer the question, follow the format, refuse what should be refused.

*Generative pretraining* (GPT-1, Radford et al. 2018) was the idea that a model pretrained on unlabelled text is a better starting point for supervised tasks than a randomly initialized one. GPT-1 was fine-tuned separately for each task. GPT-2 showed that a larger model could do some tasks with no fine-tuning, by phrasing them as text to continue, and GPT-3 that it could do many tasks *in context*: shown a few examples in the prompt, it continues the pattern, with no change to its weights (Brown et al. 2020).

*Supervised fine-tuning* (SFT) continues training on demonstrations of the desired behaviour, such as prompts paired with good responses, with the same cross-entropy loss, usually counted only on the response tokens. *Preference tuning* then optimizes the model against comparisons between responses, by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or by direct preference optimization; its machinery is the policy gradient of week 13.

:::thm Empirical | Fine-tuning shapes behaviour cheaply (Ouyang et al. 2022)
Human labelers preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT, GPT-3 fine-tuned on demonstrations and then by RLHF, over those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 it started from. The fine-tuning data were tiny compared with pretraining: tens of thousands of examples.
:::

## 4. Data: mixture, filtering, deduplication

The training corpus is the other half of the model. Web text is plentiful and mostly poor: boilerplate, spam, duplicates, machine-generated pages. A pretraining pipeline typically extracts text from crawled pages, filters by language, removes low-quality documents with heuristics and with classifiers trained to recognize reference text, deduplicates at the level of documents and of repeated spans, removes personal data and test sets, and finally mixes sources with chosen weights.

:::thm Empirical | Mixtures and filtering
GPT-3's training mix (Brown et al. 2020) sampled filtered Common Crawl 60% of the time, WebText2 22%, two book corpora 8% each and English Wikipedia 3%, so the smaller, higher-quality sources were seen several times while most of the crawl was seen less than once. The crawl was filtered with a classifier trained to tell it apart from WebText. Llama 1 trained on 1.0 to 1.4 trillion tokens of publicly available data, Llama 2 on 2 trillion and Llama 3 on over 15 trillion (Touvron et al. 2023a and b; Grattafiori et al. 2024).
:::

:::thm Empirical | Deduplication (Lee et al. 2022; Carlini et al. 2021)
Common corpora contain many near-duplicate documents and long repeated passages. Removing them let models reach the same or better accuracy with fewer training steps, and made them emit memorized text far less often. Memorization is not hypothetical: Carlini et al. extracted hundreds of verbatim training sequences from GPT-2, including personal information, by sampling from it and ranking samples by likelihood.
:::

## 5. Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020) trained hundreds of transformers and found that the test loss follows power laws over several orders of magnitude: in the number of parameters $N$ when data is unlimited, in the number of tokens $D$ when the model is large, and in the compute $C$ when both are chosen well. The architecture's shape mattered little at fixed $N$. Hoffmann et al. (2022) fitted the whole surface.

:::thm Heuristic | A training run costs about 6ND operations
A forward pass costs about $2N$ floating-point operations per token, a multiply and an add per parameter, and the backward pass about twice that, so training on $D$ tokens costs $C \approx 6ND$. This ignores attention's dependence on context length, which adds about $6\,n_{\text{layer}}n_{\text{ctx}}d$ per token and is small when $d$ is large compared with the context (Kaplan et al. 2020, table 1).
:::

:::thm Proposition | The compute-optimal split
Let $L(N, D) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}$ with $A, B, \alpha, \beta > 0$, and minimize $L$ subject to $6ND = C$. The minimizer is
$$\begin{aligned} N^*(C) &= G\Bigl(\frac C6\Bigr)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}, \quad D^*(C) = G^{-1}\Bigl(\frac C6\Bigr)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}, \\ G &= \Bigl(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}, \end{aligned}$$
and at the optimum the two reducible terms are in the ratio $\dfrac{B D^{-\beta}}{A N^{-\alpha}} = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}$.
:::proof Proof
Substitute $D = C/(6N)$: $f(N) = AN^{-\alpha} + B(6/C)^{\beta}N^{\beta}$, which is strictly convex in $\log N$ and tends to $\infty$ at both ends. Setting $f'(N) = -\alpha AN^{-\alpha-1} + \beta B(6/C)^{\beta}N^{\beta-1} = 0$ gives $N^{\alpha+\beta} = \frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}(C/6)^{\beta}$, which is the formula for $N^*$, and $D^* = C/(6N^*)$. The same equation reads $\alpha AN^{-\alpha} = \beta BD^{-\beta}$.
:::

With $\alpha \approx \beta$, both exponents are about $\frac12$: parameters and data should grow at the same rate, so the optimal number of tokens per parameter is roughly constant. Hoffmann et al. reached this with three methods, and the second experiment computes it from their fitted surface.

:::thm Empirical | Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022) and its refit (Besiroglu et al. 2024)
Hoffmann et al. estimated the exponent of $N^*$ in $C$ as 0.50 and 0.49 by two direct methods and 0.46 by fitting the surface above, with $E = 1.69$, $A = 406.4$, $B = 410.7$, $\alpha = 0.34$, $\beta = 0.28$. They trained Chinchilla, 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, with the compute of their earlier 280-billion-parameter Gopher trained on 300 billion tokens, and Chinchilla was better on nearly every evaluation. Besiroglu et al. reconstructed the data from the paper's figure and found the published surface fit inconsistent with it; their refit, $E = 1.82$, $A = 482.01$, $B = 2085.43$, $\alpha = 0.3478$, $\beta = 0.3658$, agrees with the two direct methods: about 20 tokens per parameter.
:::

:::thm Contested | Why did Kaplan et al. and Chinchilla disagree?
Kaplan et al. concluded that $N^* \propto C^{0.73}$: most extra compute should go into a bigger model, trained on relatively few tokens. GPT-3 and Gopher were sized that way. Chinchilla found about $C^{0.5}$. Proposed explanations: Kaplan et al. used one learning-rate schedule length for all runs and read off losses before the schedule had finished, which favours large models (Hoffmann et al. 2022); they counted only non-embedding parameters and worked at small scale, where the embedding is a large part of the model (Pearce and Song 2024, and the second experiment); and the cost of the last layer, the warmup length and the tuning of the optimizer at small scale bias the fits (Porian et al. 2024). These are not exclusive, and how much each contributes is still argued.
:::

Compute-optimal is not the only criterion. A model is trained once and run many times, so if inference is expensive it pays to train a smaller model on more data than the training-optimal amount (Sardana and Frankle 2024). Llama 3's 8-billion-parameter model was trained on over 15 trillion tokens, nearly 2,000 tokens per parameter.

:::thm Contested | Are abilities "emergent" with scale?
Wei et al. (2022) reported abilities, such as multi-step arithmetic, whose accuracy stays near chance below some model size and then rises sharply, unlike the smooth fall of the loss. Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) argued that many such jumps come from the metric: exact-match accuracy is a discontinuous function of per-token correctness, and with continuous metrics the same models improve smoothly. Others reply that what users care about is often exactly the discontinuous metric. Whether new capabilities can be forecast from the loss curve is unsettled.
:::

## 6. Case studies: the GPT line and Llama

:::thm Empirical | Four generations
- *GPT-1* (Radford et al. 2018): 12-layer decoder, 117 million parameters, pretrained on BooksCorpus, about 7,000 unpublished books, then fine-tuned for each task; improved the state of the art on 9 of 12 language-understanding benchmarks.
- *GPT-2* (Radford et al. 2019): 1.5 billion parameters, WebText, about 8 million web pages linked from Reddit. Zero-shot, it reached competitive results on several tasks it was never trained for.
- *GPT-3* (Brown et al. 2020): 175 billion parameters, 96 layers, context 2,048 tokens, about 300 billion training tokens. Few-shot prompting matched fine-tuned models on some benchmarks; performance with examples in the prompt improved faster with size than without.
- *Llama* (Touvron et al. 2023a): 7 to 65 billion parameters trained on public data only, 1.0 to 1.4 trillion tokens; the 13-billion-parameter model outperformed GPT-3 on most benchmarks, a direct consequence of training past the Chinchilla optimum. Its weights were released, and Llama 2 and 3 followed with chat versions and up to 405 billion parameters.
:::

## Check yourself

:::check
Derive the compute-optimal split from section 5 for $\alpha = \beta$. What fraction of a tenfold increase in compute goes into parameters, and what is the ratio of the two reducible loss terms at the optimum?
---
With $\alpha = \beta$, $N^* \propto C^{1/2}$ and $D^* \propto C^{1/2}$: ten times the compute gives $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$ times the parameters and $3.16$ times the tokens, and the ratio $D^*/N^* = G^{-2}$ does not depend on $C$. The ratio of the two reducible terms is $\alpha/\beta = 1$: at the optimum the model is held back equally by its size and by its data.
:::

:::check
Run BPE by hand on the corpus "low" ×5, "lower" ×2, "newest" ×6, "widest" ×3 (characters as base symbols, no spaces). What are the first three merges?
---
Pair counts: $e\,s$ occurs in "newest" and "widest", $6 + 3 = 9$; $s\,t$ also 9; $l\,o$ and $o\,w$ $5 + 2 = 7$; $w\,e$ $2 + 6 = 8$. The first maximum in a fixed tie-breaking order is $e\,s$ (9), giving "n e w es t", "w i d es t". Then $es\,t$ has count 9: merge to "est". Then $l\,o$ and $o\,w$ tie at 7; merging $l\,o$ gives "lo w", "lo w e r". So: $e{+}s$, $es{+}t$, $l{+}o$ (or $o{+}w$, by the tie-break).
:::

GPT-3 used about $6 \times 175\cdot10^9 \times 300\cdot10^9 \approx 3.2\cdot10^{23}$ operations. Using the refit surface, how many parameters and tokens would the compute-optimal model for that budget have?
