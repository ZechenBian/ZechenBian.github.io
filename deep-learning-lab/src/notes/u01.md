A neural network is a parametrized family of functions, and learning means choosing the parameters by walking downhill on a loss. Before any definitions, do both by hand in one variable.

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Given examples $(x_i, y_i)$ drawn from an unknown distribution, choose a function from a parametrized family $f_\theta$ that predicts $y$ from $x$ well on *new* draws, not just on the examples.
**Why can it be done this way?** Two facts carry the whole subject. Families built by composing affine maps with one fixed nonlinearity are rich enough to approximate any continuous function (section 3). And all partial derivatives of the loss cost about as much as evaluating the loss once (section 6), so descending it is feasible with millions of parameters.
**Why should it be done this way?** Because it works where nobody can write the function down: images, speech, language. That is an empirical verdict, not a theorem. Theory explains why the family is expressive; it does not yet explain why gradient descent finds good parameters, or why they generalize (section 7).
**What comes out?** A recipe that the rest of the course varies: choose an architecture (weeks 5 to 7), a loss (weeks 2 and 3), an optimizer (week 4). And two experiments: a network trained by a backward pass you can read line by line, then the same network in PyTorch on your Mac's GPU.
:::

## 1. The problem: supervised learning

The data are pairs $D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with inputs $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and targets $y_i \in Y$. The standing assumption is that they are independent draws from one distribution $P$ on $\mathbb{R}^d \times Y$; this is what gives "new data" a meaning. A *model* is a family of functions $f_\theta$ indexed by parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$, and a *loss* $\ell(\hat y, y) \ge 0$ scores a prediction against a target.

Two numbers measure a parameter value, and only one of them can be computed:

$$\begin{gathered} L_D(\theta) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \ell\bigl(f_\theta(x_i), y_i\bigr), \\ L_P(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}\, \ell\bigl(f_\theta(x), y\bigr). \end{gathered}$$

*Regression* means $Y = \mathbb{R}^m$, usually with $\ell(\hat y, y) = \lVert \hat y - y\rVert^2$. *Classification* means $Y = \{1,\dots,K\}$; the model outputs a probability vector and the loss is minus the logarithm of the probability it gave to the correct class (section 4).

For a fixed $\theta$ chosen without looking at $D$, the law of large numbers gives $L_D(\theta) \to L_P(\theta)$. But training picks $\hat\theta$ *by* minimizing $L_D$, so $L_D(\hat\theta)$ is biased downward as an estimate of $L_P(\hat\theta)$. This is why data are split three ways. The **training set** chooses $\theta$. The **validation set** chooses everything else (architecture, step size, when to stop), and whatever is chosen by looking at it becomes optimistic in the same way. The **test set** is looked at once.

## 2. The linear case, solved exactly

Take $f_W(x) = xW$ with $x$ a row vector in $\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}$ and $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}$ (a bias is absorbed by appending a coordinate equal to 1). Stack the inputs as rows of $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ and the targets as rows of $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$. The squared loss is $L_D(W) = \frac1n \lVert XW - Y\rVert_F^2$.

:::thm Proposition | Normal equations
$W$ minimizes $L_D$ if and only if $X^\top X\, W = X^\top Y$. If $X$ has full column rank the minimizer is unique, $\widehat W = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top Y$. In general the minimizers form an affine space, and the one of least Frobenius norm is $X^+ Y$, with $X^+$ the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse.
:::proof Proof
Column by column, $XW$ ranges over the column space of $X$, and the point of a subspace closest to $Y$ is the orthogonal projection, characterized by the residual being orthogonal to the subspace: $X^\top (Y - XW) = 0$. Any two solutions differ by a matrix with columns in $\ker X$; the solution with columns in $(\ker X)^\perp$ has least norm, and that is $X^+Y$.
:::

This is the only model in the course whose training is linear algebra. Everything nonlinear gives up the closed form and convexity, and gains expressive power.

One feature of the linear case will matter later. When $d > n$ there are more parameters than examples. If the rows of $X$ are linearly independent, as they are for inputs in general position, the minimizers are not unique and all of them fit the data exactly. Which one you get depends on the algorithm.

:::thm Proposition | Gradient descent prefers the least-norm interpolant
Gradient descent on $L_D(W) = \frac1n\lVert XW - Y\rVert_F^2$ started at $W = 0$, with a step size below $n/\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)$, converges to $X^+Y$.
:::proof Proof
The gradient $\frac2n X^\top(XW - Y)$ has columns in the row space of $X$, so every iterate does too. On that subspace the loss is a strictly convex quadratic, gradient descent converges to its unique minimizer there (week 4, section 1), and the unique minimizer with columns in the row space is $X^+Y$.
:::

So an optimizer can select a particular solution without being asked to. This "implicit bias" is the first hint of an answer to the puzzle in section 7.

## 3. Neural networks: the family

:::thm Definition | Feedforward network
Fix widths $n_0 = d, n_1, \dots, n_L$ and an activation $h\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, applied coordinate by coordinate. With row vectors and $a^0 = x$, set for $\ell = 1, \dots, L$
$$\begin{gathered} z^\ell = a^{\ell-1} W^\ell + b^\ell, \qquad a^\ell = h(z^\ell) \ \ (\ell < L), \\ f_\theta(x) = z^L . \end{gathered}$$
Here $W^\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell-1}\times n_\ell}$, $b^\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times n_\ell}$, and $\theta$ collects all of them: $p = \sum_\ell (n_{\ell-1} + 1)\, n_\ell$ parameters. $L$ is the depth, the $n_\ell$ are the widths, the $a^\ell$ are the activations and the $z^\ell$ the pre-activations.
:::

:::thm Convention | Rows, not columns
These notes follow the course's lecture notes: vectors are rows and $W^\ell$ multiplies on the right. PyTorch stores the transpose. `nn.Linear(n_in, n_out).weight` has shape `(n_out, n_in)` and the layer computes $x W^\top + b$.
:::

The default activation is $\mathrm{ReLU}(t) = \max(t, 0)$; others are $\tanh$, the logistic sigmoid, and GELU (week 7). Without an activation the composition collapses to a single affine map, so depth means nothing without nonlinearity.

With ReLU, $f_\theta$ is continuous and piecewise linear. In one variable with one hidden layer it is exactly what the figure at the top drew: $f(x) = c + \sum_j v_j\, \mathrm{ReLU}(w_j x + b_j)$, a sum of hinges with kinks at $-b_j / w_j$.

:::thm Theorem | Universal approximation (Cybenko 1989; Leshno, Lin, Pinkus, Schocken 1993)
Let $h\colon \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous. The set of one-hidden-layer networks
$$\begin{gathered} \Bigl\{\, x \mapsto \sum_{j=1}^N v_j\, h(x\cdot w_j + b_j) \ : \\ N \in \mathbb{N},\ v_j, b_j \in \mathbb{R},\ w_j \in \mathbb{R}^d \Bigr\} \end{gathered}$$
is dense in $C(K)$ with the uniform norm for every compact $K \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ if and only if $h$ is not a polynomial.
:::proof Proof for ReLU in one variable, and the idea in general
**One variable, ReLU.** A continuous $g$ on $[a,b]$ is uniformly continuous, so its piecewise linear interpolants on finer and finer grids converge to it uniformly. And a continuous piecewise linear function on $[a,b]$ with kinks $t_1 < \dots < t_N$ and successive slopes $s_0, s_1, \dots, s_N$ equals $g(a) + s_0\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - a) + \sum_{j=1}^N (s_j - s_{j-1})\, \mathrm{ReLU}(x - t_j)$: each hinge changes the slope by the right amount at the right place. If $g$ is twice differentiable, interpolation on a grid of $N$ cells has error at most $\lVert g''\rVert_\infty (b-a)^2 / (8N^2)$.

**Several variables.** Functions of the form $x \mapsto \varphi(x\cdot w)$ with $\varphi$ continuous span a dense subspace of $C(K)$: the powers $(x\cdot w)^k$ already span all polynomials, which are dense by Weierstrass. So it is enough to approximate each one-variable $\varphi$ on a compact interval by sums $\sum_j v_j h(w_j t + b_j)$. If $h$ is smooth, $\frac{d^k}{dw^k} h(wt + b)\big|_{w=0} = t^k h^{(k)}(b)$ is a limit of difference quotients of members of the family, and $h^{(k)}(b) \ne 0$ for some $b$ because $h$ is not a polynomial; so every monomial $t^k$ is in the closure and Weierstrass finishes. A non-smooth $h$ is first convolved with a smooth bump, which stays in the closure (for ReLU the result is visibly not a polynomial; in general this step needs a Baire category argument). **Necessity:** if $h$ is a polynomial of degree $m$, the family lies in the polynomials of degree at most $m$, a finite-dimensional and therefore closed proper subspace.
:::

What the theorem does not say is as important as what it says.

- It asserts that good weights exist. It says nothing about finding them; that is the job of sections 5 and 6, without a guarantee.
- It gives no rate. Under smoothness assumptions rates exist and degrade with dimension: for targets with $s$ bounded derivatives in $d$ variables, $N$ parameters give uniform error of order $N^{-s/d}$, and no method that picks its parameters continuously in the target does better (DeVore, Howard, Micchelli 1989). In high dimension, density alone is cheap.
- One hidden layer already suffices, so the theorem cannot explain depth.

Depth has its own, sharper statement.

:::thm Proposition | Depth buys linear pieces exponentially
Let $T(x) = 2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x) - 4\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - \tfrac12)$, the tent map on $[0,1]$. Its $k$-fold composition $T^{\circ k}$ is computed by a ReLU network with $k$ hidden layers of width 2, and any one-hidden-layer ReLU network equal to it on $[0,1]$ has at least $2^k - 1$ hidden units.
:::proof Proof
$T$ maps $[0,1]$ onto itself twice, so $T^{\circ k}$ is a sawtooth with $2^k$ linear pieces. A one-hidden-layer network in one variable with $N$ units has at most $N$ kinks, hence at most $N+1$ pieces.
:::

:::fig sawtooth | The tent map and its compositions on [0, 1]. Each further layer of two units folds the interval once more and doubles the number of pieces; a single hidden layer must pay one unit for every kink.

Telgarsky (2016) upgrades "equal" to "approximate": shallow networks need exponentially many units to come close in $L^1$. Why real data should resemble such compositions is not something a theorem tells you.

## 4. The loss for classification: softmax and cross-entropy

For $K$ classes the network outputs $z \in \mathbb{R}^K$, called *logits*. The map

$$\mathrm{softmax}(z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$$

sends them to a point of the open simplex; it is unchanged when a constant is added to every $z_k$. The loss of logits $z$ on an example with label $y$ is the *cross-entropy*

$$\ell(z, y) = -\ln \mathrm{softmax}(z)_y = -z_y + \ln \sum_j e^{z_j},$$

measured in nats because the logarithm is natural.

:::thm Proposition | Three facts about cross-entropy
Write $p = \mathrm{softmax}(z)$ and $e_y$ for the $y$-th basis vector.
1. $\nabla_z \ell = p - e_y$.
2. $\ell$ is convex in $z$, with Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(p) - p^\top p \succeq 0$, the covariance matrix of a one-hot random vector with law $p$.
3. $n\,L_D$ is the negative log-likelihood of the observed labels under the model. For a single input $x$, the expected loss when the label is drawn from the true conditional law $q(\cdot \mid x)$ is $H(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q \,\Vert\, p) \ge H(q)$, with equality exactly when $p = q$.
:::proof Proof
(1) Differentiate: $\partial_{z_k}\bigl(-z_y + \ln\sum_j e^{z_j}\bigr) = -\delta_{ky} + p_k$. (2) $\partial p_k / \partial z_j = p_k(\delta_{kj} - p_j)$, and for any $v$ the quadratic form is $\sum_k p_k v_k^2 - (\sum_k p_k v_k)^2 = \operatorname{Var}_p(v) \ge 0$. (3) is the definition of likelihood together with Gibbs' inequality (week 2).
:::

Fact 1 is why the pair softmax plus cross-entropy is used: the gradient at the output is simply "predicted probabilities minus the truth". Fact 3 says what minimizing it means: maximum likelihood (week 3). Fact 2 is about the logits only. As a function of $\theta$ the loss is not convex once there is a hidden layer. The smallest example is $f_{v,w}(x) = vwx$ fitted to the single point $(1,1)$: the loss $(vw - 1)^2$ has a hyperbola of minimizers and a saddle at the origin.

## 5. Gradient descent

Choose a step size $\eta > 0$ (the *learning rate*) and iterate $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t)$.

:::thm Proposition | Descent lemma
Suppose $\nabla L$ is $\beta$-Lipschitz. Then one step $\theta^+ = \theta - \eta \nabla L(\theta)$ satisfies
$$L(\theta^+) \le L(\theta) - \eta\Bigl(1 - \frac{\eta\beta}{2}\Bigr) \lVert \nabla L(\theta)\rVert^2 .$$
So for $0 < \eta < 2/\beta$ the loss strictly decreases unless $\nabla L(\theta) = 0$, and with $\eta = 1/\beta$ the smallest gradient seen in $T$ steps obeys $\min_{t<T} \lVert\nabla L(\theta_t)\rVert^2 \le 2\beta\,(L(\theta_0) - \inf L)/T$.
:::proof Proof
$L(\theta^+) - L(\theta) = \int_0^1 \langle \nabla L(\theta + s(\theta^+ - \theta)),\, \theta^+ - \theta\rangle\, ds \le \langle \nabla L(\theta), \theta^+ - \theta\rangle + \frac\beta2 \lVert\theta^+ - \theta\rVert^2$; substitute $\theta^+ - \theta = -\eta\nabla L(\theta)$. For the last claim, $\eta = 1/\beta$ gives $L(\theta_{t+1}) \le L(\theta_t) - \frac{1}{2\beta}\lVert\nabla L(\theta_t)\rVert^2$; sum over $t$.
:::

The lemma promises a point where the gradient is small, and nothing more: without convexity there is no guarantee of a global minimum. It also ties the step size to curvature: for $\eta < 2/\beta$ the loss is guaranteed to go down. That is a sufficient condition, and beyond it the lemma promises nothing. On a quadratic the threshold is sharp: week 4 shows the iteration diverging as soon as $\eta$ exceeds $2/\lambda_{\max}$. A ReLU network is not differentiable everywhere, so no global $\beta$ exists for it. Read the lemma as a guide to what to expect, not as a description of practice.

**Stochastic gradient descent.** $L_D$ is an average over $n$ examples. A *minibatch* $B$ of examples drawn uniformly gives the estimate $g_B = \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla \ell_i$, which is unbiased, $\mathbb{E}\, g_B = \nabla L_D$, with covariance proportional to $1/|B|$. A step now costs $|B|$ gradient evaluations instead of $n$. One pass through the data is an *epoch*. With step sizes that satisfy $\sum_t \eta_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty$, and under smoothness and bounded-variance assumptions, the gradient along the trajectory tends to zero almost surely (Robbins and Monro 1951 for the original scheme; Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 2000 in this form). In practice the step size follows a fixed schedule and training stops when the validation loss stops improving.

## 6. Backpropagation

The task is to compute $\nabla_\theta L$ for a network with $p$ parameters. Perturbing one parameter at a time takes $p + 1$ evaluations of the loss, each of cost about $p$. Backpropagation gets all $p$ derivatives for the cost of a few evaluations.

Fix one example $(x, y)$. The forward pass computes $a^0 = x$, then every $z^\ell$ and $a^\ell$, then the loss $\ell = \ell(z^L, y)$. Define the row vector $\delta^\ell = \nabla_{z^\ell}\, \ell \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times n_\ell}$.

:::thm Theorem | Backpropagation
$$\begin{gathered} \delta^L = \nabla_z \ell(z^L, y), \\ \delta^\ell = \bigl(\delta^{\ell+1} (W^{\ell+1})^\top\bigr) \odot h'(z^\ell) \quad (\ell < L), \end{gathered}$$
$$\nabla_{W^\ell}\, \ell = (a^{\ell-1})^\top \delta^\ell, \qquad \nabla_{b^\ell}\, \ell = \delta^\ell .$$
For softmax with cross-entropy, $\delta^L = p - e_y$. For a batch of $B$ examples stored as rows, with $A^{\ell-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times n_{\ell-1}}$ and $\Delta^\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times n_\ell}$, the same formulas give $\nabla_{W^\ell} L = (A^{\ell-1})^\top \Delta^\ell$ and $\nabla_{b^\ell} L = \mathbf{1}^\top \Delta^\ell$, where $\Delta^L$ carries the factor $1/B$ of the mean.
:::proof Proof
This is the chain rule, organized. Since $z^{\ell+1} = h(z^\ell)\, W^{\ell+1} + b^{\ell+1}$, we have $\partial z^{\ell+1}_k / \partial z^\ell_j = h'(z^\ell_j)\, W^{\ell+1}_{jk}$, hence $\delta^\ell_j = \sum_k \delta^{\ell+1}_k W^{\ell+1}_{jk}\, h'(z^\ell_j)$. Since $z^\ell_k = \sum_i a^{\ell-1}_i W^\ell_{ik} + b^\ell_k$, we get $\partial \ell / \partial W^\ell_{ik} = a^{\ell-1}_i \delta^\ell_k$ and $\partial \ell / \partial b^\ell_k = \delta^\ell_k$.
:::

:::fig backprop | The forward pass (left to right) stores each layer's input and pre-activation. The backward pass (right to left) carries one row vector, δ. Each weight gradient is an outer product: what arrived from the left, times what came back from the right.

The backward pass uses the same matrices as the forward pass, transposed, plus one outer product per layer: its cost is a small multiple of the forward pass, whatever $p$ is. This is an instance of a general fact.

:::thm Theorem | Cheap gradient principle (Linnainmaa 1970; Baur and Strassen 1983)
If $F\colon \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ is computed by an arithmetic circuit of size $s$, then $F$ together with all $p$ of its first partial derivatives is computed by a circuit of size $O(s)$, with a constant that does not depend on $p$.
:::proof Idea
Reverse-mode differentiation: visit the gates of the circuit in reverse order, keeping for each gate $v$ the number $\partial F / \partial v$. Each gate adds $O(1)$ operations to update the numbers of its inputs. Backpropagation is this algorithm run on the network's computation.
:::

The asymmetry between inputs and outputs matters. One backward sweep computes a vector–Jacobian product $u^\top J$; it yields the full gradient because the loss is a scalar. Propagating derivatives forward instead (one sweep per parameter) costs $p$ sweeps. A map $\mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^m$ needs $\min(p, m)$ sweeps for its full Jacobian.

**Autograd.** PyTorch records the operations of the forward pass and applies reverse mode to the record: `loss.backward()` fills `.grad` for every parameter, and nobody writes $\delta$ by hand. The second experiment checks autograd against the theorem's formulas on the same numbers.

:::thm Convention | The derivative of ReLU at 0
Implementations use $h'(0) = 0$. Where no pre-activation is exactly zero the network is differentiable in $\theta$ and backpropagation returns its gradient. On the measure-zero set of kinks the returned vector need not be a gradient or even a generalized one: for $\mathrm{ReLU}(x) - \mathrm{ReLU}(-x) = x$ the rule gives derivative $0$ at $x = 0$ instead of $1$ (see Kakade and Lee 2018). Exact zeros do occur in floating point, and the first experiment's log reports them: start it from all zeros to see the message.
:::

**Checking a gradient.** The central difference $(L(\theta + \varepsilon e_j) - L(\theta - \varepsilon e_j)) / 2\varepsilon$ equals $\partial_j L$ up to a truncation error of order $\varepsilon^2$ and a rounding error of order $u/\varepsilon$, where $u \approx 10^{-16}$ is the precision of 64-bit floats. The two balance near $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-5}$, where both are around $10^{-10}$. This test is the standard way to catch a wrong backward pass, and the first thing the first experiment does. With ReLU the test itself can fail without any bug: if the step $\varepsilon$ moves some pre-activation across $0$, the loss has a kink inside the interval and the difference quotient need not be close to the derivative at its midpoint. The experiment detects those coordinates, leaves them out and counts them.

## 7. What the theory does not explain: fitting versus generalizing

The classical picture: a richer family lowers the training loss, and past some point raises the test loss, because the model starts to fit noise. Choose the capacity at the sweet spot, or penalize complexity (week 4).

:::thm Empirical | Networks memorize noise (Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht, Vinyals 2017)
Standard image classifiers trained by stochastic gradient descent reach zero training error on CIFAR-10 after the labels are replaced by uniformly random ones, and still fit about 95% of random labels on ImageNet. With the true labels, the same architectures and the same training procedure generalize well.
:::

So no measure of capacity that depends on the family alone can explain why these models generalize: the same family does both.

:::thm Empirical | Double descent (Belkin, Hsu, Ma, Mandal 2019; Nakkiran et al. 2020)
For several model families, test error as a function of model size follows the classical U-shape, peaks near the size at which the model can just interpolate the training set, and then decreases again as the model grows further.
:::

:::thm Contested | Why do overparametrized networks generalize?
Partial explanations: the optimizer's implicit bias toward small-norm or large-margin solutions, which is proved for linear models (section 2; Soudry et al. 2018 for logistic regression on separable data); flatness of the minima that stochastic gradient descent finds (week 4 follows that debate); structure in real data. None is a complete theory. This is open research, and any one-line explanation deserves suspicion.
:::

## Check yourself

:::check
All weights and biases are initialized to exactly zero. Which parameters receive a nonzero gradient at the first step, and what does the network end up learning?
---
With zero weights every hidden activation is $h(0) = 0$ (for ReLU and for $\tanh$), the logits are $0$ and $p$ is uniform. Then $\delta^L = p - e_y \ne 0$, so $\nabla_{b^L} \ne 0$; but $\nabla_{W^L} = (a^{L-1})^\top\delta^L = 0$ because $a^{L-1} = 0$, and $\delta^{L-1} = (\delta^L (W^L)^\top)\odot h' = 0$ because $W^L = 0$, so all earlier gradients vanish too. By induction the weights stay at zero forever and only the last bias learns: the output is a constant, the class frequencies. The loss tends to the entropy of the labels, $\ln 3$ for three balanced classes. Preset 2 of the first experiment shows it. More generally, units of a layer that start equal stay equal; random initialization is there to break that symmetry.
:::

:::check
A network has $p = 10^6$ parameters and a forward pass takes time $\tau$. Estimate the cost of the full gradient by (a) finite differences, (b) forward-mode differentiation, (c) backpropagation.
---
(a) $p + 1$ forward passes: about $10^6\,\tau$. (b) One sweep per parameter, each a small multiple of $\tau$: a few times $10^6\,\tau$. (c) One forward and one backward pass: a few $\tau$. The factor of a million is the difference between impossible and routine.
:::

:::check
How many hidden ReLU units does a one-hidden-layer network need to represent the hat of the first figure exactly? And the sawtooth $T^{\circ 5}$?
---
The hat has three kinks and each unit contributes at most one, so three, and three suffice: $2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x + \tfrac12) - 4\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x) + 2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - \tfrac12)$. The sawtooth $T^{\circ 5}$ has $2^5 = 32$ pieces, so at least $31$ units in one layer, against $10$ units arranged as five layers of width 2.
:::
