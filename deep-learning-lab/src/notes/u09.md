:::lede
The models so far predict labels or next tokens. A generative model of images has a harder job: produce new images that look as if they came from the training distribution, without a formula for that distribution. This week covers two ways to learn one, a game between two networks and a lower bound on the likelihood, and the gradient trick that makes the second trainable. Start with the bound, in a model small enough to compute everything.
:::

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Learn a distribution from samples well enough to draw new samples from it: images, sounds, molecules. Two ways: a generative adversarial network (GAN) and a variational autoencoder (VAE).
**Why can it be done this way?** A network $G$ can push simple noise $z$ to a complicated distribution of $G(z)$. A GAN trains $G$ against a discriminator, and the game's optimum is the data distribution (section 2). A VAE has a latent-variable model whose likelihood is intractable, but the evidence lower bound is computable, and the reparametrization trick gives it low-variance gradients (sections 3 and 4).
**Why should it be done this way?** Each objective is a divergence between model and data, Jensen–Shannon for the GAN and KL for the VAE, so its optimum is the right distribution. GANs need no likelihood and give sharp samples; VAEs come with an encoder and a bound on the likelihood, and train stably.
**What comes out?** Photorealistic faces, unpaired image translation, and partly interpretable latent spaces (section 5); week 10's diffusion models are a VAE with many layers of latents. Two experiments: what the GAN's divergence does when the distributions do not overlap, with a GAN on eight Gaussians, and the variance of the two gradient estimators with the gap in the ELBO.
:::

## 1. Learning a distribution you can sample from

Week 2's models were explicit: they gave a probability for each sequence. For images, a model can instead be *implicit*: a sampler $x = G_\theta(z)$, $z \sim N(0, I)$, with no formula for the density of $x$, which may not even exist when $z$ has fewer dimensions than $x$. Implicit models are cheap to sample and flexible, but they cannot be trained by maximum likelihood, since the likelihood is unavailable. GANs train such a sampler directly. VAEs keep a likelihood, $p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)\,dz$, and replace the integral by a bound.

## 2. Generative adversarial networks

:::fig ganfig | A GAN. The generator turns noise into samples; the discriminator is trained to tell real from fake; the generator is trained, through the discriminator's gradient, to make its samples pass as real.

:::thm Definition | The GAN game (Goodfellow et al. 2014)
A generator $G\colon\mathcal Z \to \mathcal X$ with $z \sim p_z$ induces a distribution $p_g$. A discriminator $D\colon\mathcal X \to (0,1)$ estimates the probability that its input is real. The value of the game is
$$\begin{aligned} V(D, G) &= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\log D(x) \\ &\quad + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}\log\bigl(1 - D(G(z))\bigr), \end{aligned}$$
and training seeks $\min_G\max_D V(D,G)$, alternating gradient steps on $D$ and on $G$.
:::

:::thm Proposition | The optimal discriminator
For fixed $G$, with densities $p_{\text{data}}$ and $p_g$, $V$ is maximized by $D^*(x) = \dfrac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}$.
:::proof Proof
$V(D, G) = \int \bigl(p_{\text{data}}(x)\log D(x) + p_g(x)\log(1 - D(x))\bigr)dx$. For $a, b \ge 0$ not both 0, $y \mapsto a\log y + b\log(1 - y)$ on $(0,1)$ is maximized at $y = a/(a+b)$; maximize pointwise.
:::

:::thm Theorem | What the generator minimizes
With the optimal discriminator, $\max_D V(D, G) = -\log 4 + 2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g)$, where $\mathrm{JS}(p\,\|\,q) = \frac12 D(p\,\|\,m) + \frac12 D(q\,\|\,m)$ with $m = \frac12(p + q)$. So the global minimum over $G$ is attained exactly when $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$.
:::proof Proof
Substitute $D^*$: $V = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\log\frac{p_{\text{data}}}{2m} + \mathbb{E}_{p_g}\log\frac{p_g}{2m} = -2\log 2 + D(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,m) + D(p_g\,\|\,m)$. The JS divergence is nonnegative and vanishes only when the two distributions are equal (Gibbs' inequality, week 2).
:::

The theorem describes the game at its optimum. Training is different: $D$ is a network trained for a few steps, $G$ moves against a moving target, and the two-player dynamics need not converge. Two practical changes appear in almost every implementation.

:::thm Heuristic | The non-saturating generator loss
Early in training the discriminator rejects fakes confidently, $D(G(z)) \approx 0$. Minimizing $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$ then gives the generator almost no gradient: with $D = \varsigma(\ell)$, $\frac{d}{d\ell}\log(1 - \varsigma(\ell)) = -\varsigma(\ell) \approx 0$. Goodfellow et al. instead let $G$ maximize $\log D(G(z))$, whose derivative $1 - \varsigma(\ell) \approx 1$ is large exactly then. It has the same fixed points but a different game, and is what the first experiment uses.
:::

A deeper problem is the divergence itself.

:::thm Proposition | JS is blind to distance between non-overlapping distributions
If $p$ and $q$ have disjoint supports, then $\mathrm{JS}(p\,\|\,q) = \log 2$, whatever the distance between the supports. So if $p_g$ and $p_{\text{data}}$ do not overlap, the generator's objective with an optimal discriminator is constant, and its gradient is zero.
:::proof Proof
On the support of $p$, $m = \frac12 p$, so $D(p\,\|\,m) = \mathbb{E}_p\log 2 = \log 2$; likewise $D(q\,\|\,m) = \log 2$.
:::

Natural images lie near a low-dimensional subset of pixel space, and so does the output of a generator with low-dimensional $z$. Two such sets generically do not intersect, so this is the usual situation at the start of training (Arjovsky and Bottou 2017). The first experiment shows the near-disjoint case: two narrow Gaussians, whose JS divergence is $\ln 2$ to six digits at shifts 1 and 2.

:::thm Theorem | Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality (Villani 2009, chapter 5)
For distributions with finite first moments, the Wasserstein-1 distance $W_1(p, q) = \inf_\gamma \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\gamma}\lvert x - y\rvert$, the infimum over couplings $\gamma$ of $p$ and $q$, equals
$$W_1(p, q) = \sup_{\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}}\le 1}\ \mathbb{E}_p f - \mathbb{E}_q f.$$
:::

The Wasserstein GAN (Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou 2017) replaces the discriminator by a 1-Lipschitz *critic* $f$ and the JS divergence by $W_1$, which shrinks continuously as the distributions approach; for two Gaussians differing by a shift, it is the length of the shift. The Lipschitz constraint was first enforced by clipping weights, later by penalizing the critic's gradient norm (Gulrajani et al. 2017).

:::thm Empirical | Mode collapse and what GANs learn
A generator can satisfy a discriminator by producing a few convincing kinds of output and ignoring the rest of the distribution, since nothing in the game rewards diversity directly. Arora and Zhang (2017) estimated the support size of trained GANs with a birthday-paradox test: in batches of a few hundred face images they often found near-duplicates, which suggests far smaller diversity than the training set. Arora et al. (2017) showed that with a discriminator of finite capacity, a generator of small support can be near-optimal.
:::

## 3. Variational autoencoders and the evidence lower bound

A latent-variable model has a prior $p(z)$, typically $N(0, I)$, and a decoder $p_\theta(x\mid z)$. The likelihood $p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz$ is an integral over all latent values, intractable for a neural decoder, and so is the posterior $p_\theta(z\mid x)$.

:::thm Proposition | The evidence lower bound
For any distribution $q(z)$ with $q \ll p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$,
$$\begin{aligned} \log p_\theta(x) &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\log p_\theta(x, z) - \log q(z)\bigr]}_{\mathrm{ELBO}(q)} \\ &\quad + \mathrm{KL}\bigl(q\,\big\|\,p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\bigr). \end{aligned}$$
Hence $\mathrm{ELBO}(q) \le \log p_\theta(x)$, with equality if and only if $q = p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$. Equivalently, $\mathrm{ELBO}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q\log p_\theta(x\mid z) - \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$.
:::proof Proof
For every $z$, $\log p_\theta(x) = \log p_\theta(x, z) - \log p_\theta(z\mid x) = \bigl[\log p_\theta(x,z) - \log q(z)\bigr] + \bigl[\log q(z) - \log p_\theta(z\mid x)\bigr]$. Take the expectation over $z \sim q$: the left side does not depend on $z$, the first bracket gives the ELBO and the second the KL divergence, which is nonnegative and vanishes only when $q$ is the posterior (Gibbs' inequality). The second form follows from $\log p_\theta(x,z) = \log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z)$.
:::

The figure at the top showed this in a model with a known posterior: no choice of $q$ pushes the bound above $\log p(x)$, and the gap closes when $q$ is the posterior. In general, maximizing the ELBO over $q$ is *variational inference*: it minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$, the mode-seeking direction of week 2.

:::fig vaefig | A variational autoencoder. The encoder outputs the parameters of q(z | x); a latent is formed as μ + σ ⊙ ε with external noise ε; the decoder reconstructs x. The loss is the negative ELBO: reconstruction error plus a KL penalty that keeps q close to the prior.

:::thm Definition | Variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling 2014; Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra 2014)
An *encoder* network outputs $\mu_\phi(x)$ and $\sigma_\phi(x)$, defining $q_\phi(z\mid x) = N\bigl(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi(x)^2\bigr)$; a *decoder* network defines $p_\theta(x\mid z)$. Both are trained by maximizing $\sum_i \mathrm{ELBO}_{\theta,\phi}(x_i)$, with the expectation over $q$ estimated by one or a few samples. One encoder serves all data points: *amortized* inference.
:::

:::thm Proposition | The KL term in closed form
$\mathrm{KL}\bigl(N(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2)\,\big\|\,N(0, I)\bigr) = \frac12\sum_{j}\bigl(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_j^2\bigr)$.
:::proof Proof
Both are products over coordinates, so the divergence is a sum. In one coordinate, $\mathbb{E}_q[\log q - \log p] = -\frac12\log\sigma^2 - \frac12 + \frac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2)$, using $\mathbb{E}_q(z - \mu)^2 = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb{E}_q z^2 = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$.
:::

With a Gaussian decoder of fixed variance, the reconstruction term is a squared error, so a VAE is an autoencoder whose code is noisy and pulled towards the prior. Two well-known weaknesses follow from the objective. Samples tend to be blurry, since the decoder's mean averages over the uncertainty in $z$. And with a decoder powerful enough to model $x$ without $z$, such as an autoregressive one, the optimum can ignore the latent altogether, setting $q(z\mid x) = p(z)$: *posterior collapse* (Bowman et al. 2016).

## 4. The reparametrization trick

Training the encoder needs the gradient with respect to $\phi$ of $\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\phi}f(z)$, where $f(z) = \log p_\theta(x, z) - \log q_\phi(z)$. The distribution depends on $\phi$, so the gradient cannot simply be moved inside a sample average.

:::thm Proposition | Two unbiased estimators
Let $q_\mu = N(\mu, I)$ and $f$ differentiable with suitable growth. Then
$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\mu\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\mu}f(z) &= \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim N(0,I)}\nabla f(\mu + \varepsilon) \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\mu}\bigl[f(z)\,\nabla_\mu\log q_\mu(z)\bigr]. \end{aligned}$$
The first is the *reparametrization* (pathwise) estimator, the second the *score-function* (REINFORCE, likelihood-ratio) estimator; here $\nabla_\mu\log q_\mu(z) = z - \mu$. For any constant $b$, replacing $f$ by $f - b$ in the second keeps it unbiased.
:::proof Proof
Write $z = \mu + \varepsilon$ and differentiate under the integral over $\varepsilon$, whose distribution does not depend on $\mu$. For the second, differentiate $\int f(z)q_\mu(z)\,dz$ under the integral and use $\nabla_\mu q_\mu = q_\mu\nabla_\mu\log q_\mu$. A baseline is harmless because $\mathbb{E}_{q_\mu}\nabla_\mu\log q_\mu = \nabla_\mu\int q_\mu = 0$.
:::

Both are unbiased; their variances differ enormously. In one dimension with $f(z) = (z - 2)^2$ and $\mu = 0$, the true derivative is $-4$. The pathwise estimator is $2(z - 2)$, with variance 4. The score-function estimator is $(z-2)^2 z$, whose variance is $\mathbb{E}[z^2(z-2)^4] - 16 = 103 - 16 = 87$, using the Gaussian moments $1, 3, 15$ of $z^2, z^4, z^6$; with the baseline $b = \mathbb{E}f = 5$ it is $58 - 16 = 42$. The second experiment measures these, and their growth with dimension.

:::thm Heuristic | Why the pathwise gradient wins
The pathwise estimator uses the derivative of $f$ at the sample, information the score function never sees: the score function must infer the direction of improvement from the correlation between values of $f$ and random directions, and every coordinate of $z$ that $f$ depends on adds noise to that correlation. So its variance grows with the dimension, while the pathwise estimator's need not. The score function is still the only option when $f$ cannot be differentiated, as for discrete latent variables or a reward from an environment, which is why it reappears as the policy gradient of week 13.
:::

## 5. Case studies: image generation, CycleGAN, β-VAE

Generated images are judged by eye and by statistics of a pretrained classifier's features.

:::thm Definition | Fréchet inception distance (Heusel et al. 2017)
Fit Gaussians $N(\mu_r, \Sigma_r)$ and $N(\mu_g, \Sigma_g)$ to the features of an Inception network on real and on generated images. The FID is the squared 2-Wasserstein distance between them, $\lvert\mu_r - \mu_g\rvert^2 + \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\bigr)$; lower is better. It rewards both quality and diversity, but only as seen through one network's features.
:::

:::thm Empirical | GAN image generation
DCGAN (Radford, Metz and Chintala 2016) found convolutional architectures and training settings that made GANs train reliably on images, and showed arithmetic in latent space (smiling woman − neutral woman + neutral man ≈ smiling man). Progressive growing and then StyleGAN (Karras et al. 2018, 2019) generated faces at $1024\times1024$ that people find hard to tell from photographs. In the 2020s diffusion models (week 10) overtook GANs on most image benchmarks, while GANs remain much faster to sample from.
:::

:::thm Definition | CycleGAN (Zhu, Park, Isola and Efros 2017)
To translate between two image domains $X$ and $Y$, say horses and zebras, without paired examples, train $G\colon X \to Y$ and $F\colon Y \to X$, each with its own discriminator, and add a *cycle-consistency* loss
$$\lambda\bigl(\mathbb{E}_x\lVert F(G(x)) - x\rVert_1 + \mathbb{E}_y\lVert G(F(y)) - y\rVert_1\bigr)$$
so that translating there and back returns the original. The GAN losses make outputs look like the target domain; the cycle loss keeps them tied to their inputs.
:::

:::thm Empirical | β-VAE (Higgins et al. 2017)
Weighting the KL term of the ELBO by $\beta > 1$ pushes the encoder towards latents that are independent under $q$, at the cost of reconstruction. On datasets generated from a few factors, such as shapes varying in position, scale and rotation, individual latent coordinates of a β-VAE often came to track individual factors: a *disentangled* representation.
:::

:::thm Contested | Can disentanglement be learned without supervision? (Locatello et al. 2019)
Locatello et al. proved that for any generative model with a factorized prior there are infinitely many others, with entangled latents, that give exactly the same distribution of data, so unsupervised learning alone cannot identify the "true" factors without inductive biases on the model or the data. In a study of thousands of trained models, they found that random seeds and hyperparameters mattered more than the choice among disentangling objectives, and that good models could not be identified without some supervision. Supporters of the program answer that the right inductive biases, or weak supervision, are available in practice; how much disentanglement buys downstream is still debated.
:::

---
$D^* = \frac{1}{1 + 1/2} = \frac23$ on $[0,1]$ and $0$ on $(1, 2]$. With $m = \frac34$ on $[0,1]$ and $\frac14$ on $(1,2]$: $D(p\,\|\,m) = \log\frac43$ and $D(q\,\|\,m) = \frac12\log\frac{1/2}{3/4} + \frac12\log\frac{1/2}{1/4} = \frac12\log\frac43$, so $\mathrm{JS} = \frac34\log\frac43 \approx 0.216$ nats and $\max_D V = -\log 4 + 2 \cdot 0.216 \approx -0.955$.
:::

:::check
In the figure's model, $z \sim N(0,1)$, $x\mid z \sim N(z, 0.5)$ and $x = 1.5$. Compute the posterior, and the gap $\log p(x) - \mathrm{ELBO}(q)$ for $q = N(0, 1)$, the prior.
---

:::

:::check
Show that the score-function estimator with a baseline $b$ is unbiased, and find the constant $b$ that minimizes its variance for a scalar parameter.
---
Unbiasedness: $\mathbb{E}[(f - b)s] = \mathbb{E}[fs] - b\,\mathbb{E}[s]$ with $s = \partial\log q$, and $\mathbb{E}s = 0$. The variance is $\mathbb{E}[(f - b)^2s^2] - (\mathbb{E}fs)^2$, a quadratic in $b$ minimized at $b^* = \mathbb{E}[fs^2]/\mathbb{E}[s^2]$, a weighted average of $f$, close to $\mathbb{E}f$ when $f$ and $s^2$ are nearly uncorrelated.
