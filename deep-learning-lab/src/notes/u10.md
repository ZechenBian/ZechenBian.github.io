:::lede
A diffusion model learns to undo noise. Take data, add a little Gaussian noise a thousand times until nothing of the data is left, and train a network to remove one step of noise at a time; then start from pure noise and run the steps backwards. The training objective is week 9's evidence lower bound for a VAE with a thousand layers of latents and an encoder that has no parameters. Start with one dimension, where the best possible denoiser can be computed exactly.
:::

:::widget diffuse

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Learn a distribution from samples and draw new ones, like week 9's GANs and VAEs: images above all, also audio, video, molecules and robot motions.
**Why can it be done this way?** A fixed chain of small Gaussian steps turns any data distribution into standard noise, and each step can be undone approximately by another Gaussian step whose mean is the output of a network (sections 1 and 2). Training only asks the network to predict the noise that was added, a regression problem with a fresh target for every sample and every noise level (section 3).
**Why should it be done this way?** The loss is a weighted form of the evidence lower bound, so the model is fitted by likelihood, without a GAN's game; and denoising is easy at every level, so training is stable. The best denoiser is a conditional mean, equivalently the score $\nabla \log q_t$ of the noised data (section 4).
**What comes out?** The image generators of the last few years, Stable Diffusion among them (section 6), and samplers that trade steps for speed (section 5). Two experiments: why the exact optimal denoiser can only reproduce its training set while a trained network does not, and a diffusion model on a ring of points with three ways to sample from it.
:::

## 1. Adding noise, one step at a time

Fix a number of steps $T$ and a *noise schedule* $0 < \beta_1 < \dots < \beta_T < 1$. Write $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots\alpha_t$.

:::thm Definition | The forward process (Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015; Ho, Jain and Abbeel 2020)
Given a data point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the forward process is the Markov chain
$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = N\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\bigr)$$
for $t = 1, \dots, T$, that is, $x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon_t$ with independent $\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, I)$. It has no parameters. The factor $\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}$ keeps the variance at $1$ when the data have variance $1$.
:::

:::thm Proposition | Any step in closed form
$$\begin{aligned}q(x_t \mid x_0) &= N\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\bigr),\\ \text{that is}\quad x_t &= \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$
with $\varepsilon \sim N(0, I)$.
:::proof Proof
By induction on $t$; the case $t = 1$ is the definition. If $x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon'$, then $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}\,\varepsilon' + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon_t$. The last two terms are independent centred Gaussians, so their sum is Gaussian with covariance $\bigl(\alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t + 1 - \alpha_t\bigr)I = (1 - \bar\alpha_t)I$, and $\sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$.
:::

So training never has to run the chain: to get $x_t$, draw one $\varepsilon$. The number that matters at step $t$ is the signal-to-noise ratio $\mathrm{SNR}(t) = \bar\alpha_t/(1 - \bar\alpha_t)$, which falls from $\infty$ to nearly $0$. Ho, Jain and Abbeel took $T = 1000$ and $\beta_t$ linear from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$; then $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{1000}} \approx 0.006$, so $x_T$ is standard noise for any data of moderate size, and the widget uses this schedule. Nichol and Dhariwal (2021) replaced it by a *cosine schedule*, $\bar\alpha_t = f(t)/f(0)$ with $f(t) = \cos^2\bigl(\frac{t/T + s}{1 + s}\cdot\frac\pi2\bigr)$ and $s = 0.008$, which destroys the signal more evenly; with the linear schedule the last few hundred steps are spent on almost pure noise.

:::fig schedfig | How much of the data is left: the coefficients of x₀ and of the noise in x_t, for the linear schedule of Ho, Jain and Abbeel over 1,000 steps, and, dashed, the coefficient of x₀ under the cosine schedule.

The experiments use a short schedule, $T = 100$ with $\beta_t$ linear from $0.001$ to $0.2$, for which $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{100}} < 0.005$.

## 2. Running it backwards

To generate, we want to run the chain backwards: draw $x_T \sim N(0, I)$, then $x_{T-1}$ from $q(x_{T-1} \mid x_T)$, and so on. The reverse kernels $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ depend on the unknown data distribution. With the clean point given, they do not.

:::thm Proposition | The posterior of one step
For $t \ge 2$,
$$q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = N\bigl(\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0),\ \tilde\beta_t I\bigr),$$
$$\begin{aligned}\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) &= \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,x_t,\\ \tilde\beta_t &= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t.\end{aligned}$$
:::proof Proof
By Bayes' rule and the Markov property, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\,q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. As a function of $x_{t-1}$ the exponent is
$$-\frac{|x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}|^2}{2\beta_t} - \frac{|x_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0|^2}{2(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})},$$
a quadratic, so the posterior is Gaussian. The coefficient of $-\frac12|x_{t-1}|^2$ is the precision $\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}} = \frac{\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})} = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}$, whose inverse is $\tilde\beta_t$. The linear term is $\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t}x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0$, and the mean is $\tilde\beta_t$ times it, which is $\tilde\mu_t$.
:::

The model imitates this step without knowing $x_0$:
$$p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = N\bigl(\mu_\theta(x_t, t),\ \sigma_t^2 I\bigr),$$
with $\sigma_t^2$ fixed, $\beta_t$ or $\tilde\beta_t$, and $p(x_T) = N(0, I)$. Sampling from it, step by step from $t = T$ down to $1$, is called *ancestral sampling*.

:::thm Heuristic | Why a Gaussian reverse step is enough
The true reverse kernel $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ is a mixture of the Gaussians above over the posterior of $x_0$, not a Gaussian. When $\beta_t$ is small, $x_{t-1}$ is pinned to within about $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ of $x_t$, and over so short a distance the log-density of the data blurred to level $t - 1$ is nearly linear; a Gaussian times the exponential of a linear function is again a Gaussian. This is why the steps must be many and small, and why fast samplers that take big steps need other arguments (section 5). In the continuous-time limit the forward process becomes a stochastic differential equation whose time reversal is exact (Anderson 1982; Song et al. 2021).
:::

## 3. The evidence lower bound again

The latents $x_1, \dots, x_T$ have the same dimension as the data, the "encoder" $q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)$ is the fixed forward chain, and the "decoder" is the learned reverse chain. A diffusion model is a hierarchical VAE with $T$ layers whose encoder is not learned.

:::fig chainfig | A diffusion model as a hierarchical VAE. The forward arrows are the encoder, fixed in advance; the backward arrows are the decoder, the only part with parameters. Every layer of latents has the dimension of the data.

:::thm Proposition | The bound, term by term
For any data point $x_0$,
$$\log p_\theta(x_0) \ \ge\ \mathbb{E}_q\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1) - \sum_{t=2}^{T} L_{t-1} - L_T,$$
where
$$\begin{aligned}L_{t-1} &= \mathbb{E}_q\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\\ &\qquad\qquad \big\|\ p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\bigr),\\ L_T &= \mathrm{KL}\bigl(q(x_T \mid x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)\bigr).\end{aligned}$$
:::proof Proof sketch
Week 9's bound with $z = x_{1:T}$ is $\log p_\theta(x_0) \ge \mathbb{E}_q\bigl[\log p_\theta(x_{0:T}) - \log q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\bigr]$. Write $p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T)\prod_t p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$, and, using the Markov property and Bayes' rule, $q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) = q(x_T \mid x_0)\prod_{t=2}^T q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$. Matching the factors and taking expectations turns each ratio into a KL divergence.
:::

The term $L_T$ has no parameters. Each $L_{t-1}$ compares two Gaussians with the same covariance $\sigma_t^2 I$ up to the gap between $\tilde\beta_t$ and $\sigma_t^2$, so it is $\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta|^2$ plus a constant. Now substitute $x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ into $\tilde\mu_t$; the coefficients collapse to
$$\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Bigl(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Bigr).$$
So the natural choice is to let the network predict the noise $\varepsilon$, and set $\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\bigl(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\bigr)$.

:::thm Proposition | The bound is a weighted noise-prediction loss
With this parametrization, and $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$,
$$\begin{aligned}L_{t-1} = \ &\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\\ &\times\mathbb{E}_{x_0, \varepsilon}\bigl|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\bigr|^2 + \text{const}.\end{aligned}$$
:::proof Proof
$\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta = -\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta)$; square it and divide by $2\sigma_t^2$.
:::

:::thm Definition | The simplified loss (Ho, Jain and Abbeel 2020)
$$L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_0,\,\varepsilon}\,\bigl|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\bigr|^2,$$
with $t$ uniform on $\{1, \dots, T\}$, $x_0$ from the data, $\varepsilon \sim N(0, I)$ and $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$.
:::

It drops three things from the bound: the weights in front of each term, the constants, which depend on the choice of $\sigma_t$, and the special treatment of the last step $p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$, which in the paper is a discrete decoder for 8-bit pixels. The weights matter: with $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ they equal $\beta_t/(2\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_t))$, which is $\frac12$ at $t = 1$ and stays near $0.01$ or below over most of the schedule of Ho, Jain and Abbeel. The simplified loss gives the noisy steps, where the large structures of an image are decided, relatively more weight than the bound does.

:::thm Empirical | What the simplified loss buys
Ho, Jain and Abbeel report that training on $L_{\text{simple}}$ gave much better samples than training on the bound itself, with an FID of 3.17 on CIFAR-10, the best unconditional score at the time, and a slightly worse bound on the likelihood. Kingma et al. (2021) showed that in continuous time the bound depends on the schedule only through $\mathrm{SNR}$ at the two ends, and Kingma and Gao (2023) that a weighted loss whose weight grows with the noise level is the bound for the data with Gaussian noise of a random level added, so "simplified" is a choice of what to fit rather than a loss of rigour.
:::

## 4. The best denoiser, the score, and memorization

Every term of the loss is a least-squares regression of $\varepsilon$ on $x_t$, so its minimizer over all functions is a conditional mean.

:::thm Proposition | The optimal noise predictor
Over all measurable $f$, $\mathbb{E}|\varepsilon - f(x_t, t)|^2$ is minimized by $f^*(x, t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t = x]$. Equivalently, the denoised estimate $\hat x_0 = \bigl(x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,f^*\bigr)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ equals $\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t]$.
:::proof Proof
For any $f$, $\mathbb{E}|\varepsilon - f|^2 = \mathbb{E}|\varepsilon - f^*|^2 + \mathbb{E}|f^* - f|^2$, because the cross term vanishes by the tower property. The second statement follows by taking $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid x_t]$ of $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$.
:::

:::thm Proposition | Tweedie's formula (Robbins 1956; Efron 2011)
Let $q_t$ be the density of $x_t$. Then
$$\nabla_x \log q_t(x) = -\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t = x]}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}.$$
So a perfect noise predictor is, up to a factor, the *score* of the noised data, and a diffusion model is the same thing as a score-based model trained by denoising score matching (Vincent 2011; Song and Ermon 2019).
:::proof Proof
$q_t(x) = \int \varphi(x)\,p(dx_0)$, where $\varphi$ is the density of $N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)I)$ at $x$. Differentiate under the integral: $\nabla\varphi = -\frac{x - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\varphi$. Dividing by $q_t(x)$ gives $-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \bigm| x_t = x\bigr]$, and $x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 = \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$.
:::

A network is trained on finitely many examples $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}$, so the loss it actually minimizes is the one for their empirical distribution, and there the optimum is explicit.

:::thm Proposition | The optimal denoiser for a training set
If the data are the empirical distribution of $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}$, then
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t = x] &= \sum_{i=1}^n w_i(x)\,x^{(i)},\\ w(x) &= \mathrm{softmax}_i\Bigl(-\frac{|x - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x^{(i)}|^2}{2(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\Bigr).\end{aligned}$$
As $t \to 0$ the weights concentrate on the training point nearest to $x/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$.
:::proof Proof
Bayes' rule with the uniform prior on the $n$ points and the Gaussian likelihood $q(x_t \mid x^{(i)})$; the normalizing constants are the same for every $i$. The weights are week 7's attention weights with the noisy point as the query and the scaled training points as keys.
:::

So a model that reached the optimum at every $t$ would, sampled with small steps (section 2), return the training points and nothing else. The widget shows the staircase this produces in one dimension. The first experiment does it in the plane: 20 training points on a circle, the exact optimal denoiser against a network trained on the same 20 points with the same loss.

:::thm Contested | Why do diffusion models generalize?
If the optimum of the training loss memorizes, then a network that generalizes is one that fails to reach the optimum, in a particular way. Carlini et al. (2023) and Somepalli et al. (2023) extracted near-copies of training images from Stable Diffusion and other models, more often for images duplicated many times in the training data. Kadkhodaie et al. (2024) trained the same network on two disjoint sets of face images: with small sets each copy memorized its own, and with large ones the two copies produced nearly identical new faces from the same noise, which they attribute to the inductive bias of the network rather than to the objective. What exactly that bias is, and how much a model may copy before it counts as reproduction, are open questions, the second also a legal one.
:::

## 5. Sampling: the noise is not decoration

Ancestral sampling adds fresh noise $\sigma_t z$ at every step. That noise looks like an unwanted source of variation, and leaving it out seems a way to get "the most likely" sample. It is not.

:::thm Heuristic | Dropping the fresh noise shrinks the samples
While $\bar\alpha_t \approx 0$, $x_t$ is nearly independent of $x_0$, so the best prediction of the noise is $\varepsilon_\theta \approx x_t/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ and the mean step becomes $\mu_\theta \approx x_t\bigl(1 - \frac{\beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\bigr)/\sqrt{\alpha_t} \approx \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_t$. With the noise term the variance stays near $1$, as it must; without it, every step multiplies the sample by $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$. Over the noisy half of the experiment's schedule, where $\bar\alpha_t < 0.1$, the product of these factors is about $0.014$. The trajectory arrives at the informative steps far too close to the centre, at a place where the network was trained on almost nothing, and never recovers.
:::

:::thm Proposition | A deterministic sampler (Song, Meng and Ermon 2021)
If $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$, then $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon$ has exactly the law $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. The DDIM step replaces $x_0$ and $\varepsilon$ by the network's estimates:
$$\begin{aligned}\hat x_0 &= \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},\\ x_{t-1} &= \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t).\end{aligned}$$
:::proof Proof sketch
The first statement holds because $\varepsilon \sim N(0, I)$ is independent of $x_0$. Song, Meng and Ermon build a forward process that is not Markov but has the same marginals $q(x_t \mid x_0)$; since the loss of section 3 depends only on these marginals, the same network serves, and the reverse step of the new process with zero variance is the formula above.
:::

The difference from the mean-only sampler: DDIM puts back the predicted noise at the level of step $t - 1$, so the sample stays at the noise level the network expects. It is deterministic, so a starting noise names an image and two noises can be interpolated, and it can skip steps: Song, Meng and Ermon get good samples in tens of steps instead of a thousand. In the limit of small steps it is a discretization of an ordinary differential equation, the probability flow ODE, with the same marginals as the stochastic process (Song et al. 2021).

The second experiment trains a network on a ring of points and samples it three ways: ancestral, mean only, and DDIM.

## 6. Case study: Stable Diffusion

Running a diffusion model on 512 × 512 pixels with three colours means a denoiser with 786,432 inputs and outputs at each of many steps. Most of those numbers encode detail that the eye barely notices.

:::fig ldmfig | Latent diffusion. An autoencoder, trained first and then frozen, compresses each image to a 64 × 64 × 4 latent; diffusion runs on latents, with a U-Net that reads the prompt through cross-attention; the decoder turns the final latent into an image.

:::thm Definition | Latent diffusion (Rombach et al. 2022)
Train an autoencoder $(E, D)$ with $D(E(x)) \approx x$, with a perceptual and an adversarial loss so that the reconstructions stay sharp. Then train a diffusion model on $z_0 = E(x)$, and sample images as $D(z_0)$ with $z_0$ drawn by the reverse process. The denoiser is a U-Net, a convolutional network with skip connections between layers of the same resolution (week 5), with attention layers in which the latent's positions are queries and the encoded text $c$ supplies keys and values.
:::

Stable Diffusion, released in 2022, is this design with a downsampling factor of 8, so $512 \times 512 \times 3$ pixels become $64 \times 64 \times 4$ latents, 48 times fewer numbers; a frozen CLIP text encoder; and a U-Net of roughly 860 million parameters trained on image–caption pairs from the LAION collection. Its weights were released openly, which made it the base of much later work.

:::thm Definition | Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans 2021)
Train one network both with the prompt and, for a random fraction of examples, with an empty prompt $\varnothing$. At sampling time use
$$\begin{aligned}\tilde\varepsilon = \ &\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing)\\ &+ s\,\bigl(\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, c) - \varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing)\bigr)\end{aligned}$$
with a guidance scale $s > 1$.
By Tweedie's formula this moves along $\nabla\log p(z_t \mid c) + (s - 1)\nabla\log p(c \mid z_t)$: it samples a sharpened distribution in which images that match the prompt clearly are favoured.
:::

:::thm Empirical | Guidance trades diversity for fidelity
Larger $s$ gives images that follow the prompt more closely and look cleaner, but are less varied, more saturated and eventually distorted; Stable Diffusion's default is $s = 7.5$. Dhariwal and Nichol (2021) first showed diffusion models beating GANs on ImageNet by FID with a related method, guidance by a separately trained classifier. The guided distribution is not the data distribution, so a guided model's likelihood says little about its samples.
:::

## Check yourself

:::check
Put $t = 1$ into the posterior of section 2, with $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$. What do you get, and why is the proposition stated for $t \ge 2$?
---
The formula would give $\tilde\beta_1 = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_0}{1 - \bar\alpha_1}\beta_1 = 0$ with $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$, and $\tilde\mu_1 = x_0$: given $x_0$, there is nothing left to infer about $x_0$, and $q(x_0 \mid x_1, x_0)$ is a point mass at $x_0$. That is why the bound treats the last step separately, as a decoder $p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$ evaluated at the data.
:::

:::check
Show that the noise prediction and the clean prediction are interchangeable: if a network outputs $\hat x_0(x_t, t)$ instead, how is the term $|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta|^2$ of $L_{\text{simple}}$ expressed through $\hat x_0$, and which steps does it weight most?
---
From $\varepsilon = (x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0)/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ and the same for the estimates, $|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta|^2 = \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}|x_0 - \hat x_0|^2 = \mathrm{SNR}(t)\,|x_0 - \hat x_0|^2$. So $L_{\text{simple}}$ is a clean-image regression weighted by the signal-to-noise ratio, which is largest at small $t$; equivalently, the clean-image loss with equal weights would put more weight on the noisy steps than $L_{\text{simple}}$ does.
:::

:::check
In one dimension, let the data be $N(0, s^2)$. Find $\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t]$ and the score $\nabla\log q_t$, and check Tweedie's formula.
---
$x_t \sim N(0, v)$ with $v = \bar\alpha_t s^2 + 1 - \bar\alpha_t$, so $\nabla\log q_t(x) = -x/v$. Since $(x_0, x_t)$ is jointly Gaussian, $\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t] = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,s^2}{v}x_t$, and $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t] = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbb{E}[x_0\mid x_t]}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}} = \frac{(1 - \bar\alpha_t)\,x_t}{v\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}$. Then $-\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = -x_t/v$, as Tweedie's formula says. The best denoiser is linear here; it becomes a staircase only when the data have separated clusters, as in the widget.
:::
