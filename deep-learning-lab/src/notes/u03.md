:::lede
Week 1 used two losses, cross-entropy for classification and squared error for regression, and took them as given. Each is a statement about the data: a loss is a negative log-likelihood, and a penalty such as weight decay is a negative log-prior. This week develops the statistics behind that and asks what makes an estimator good. First a picture of what a penalty does to a minimum.
:::

:::widget ballmin

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Turn a finite sample into statements about the distribution it came from: estimate parameters, say how far off the estimate is likely to be, and compare estimators. For deep learning it explains where the standard losses and penalties come from.
**Why can it be done this way?** Averages concentrate: by the central limit theorem the error of a sample mean is approximately Gaussian with width $\sigma/\sqrt n$, whatever the distribution (section 1). A probabilistic model of the data turns into a function to optimize, the likelihood (section 2), and Bayes' rule turns a prior into an additive penalty (section 4).
**Why should it be done this way?** Maximum likelihood is asymptotically efficient: no unbiased estimator has smaller variance than the Cramér–Rao bound, and the maximum-likelihood estimator attains it as $n \to \infty$ (section 2). The posterior mean has the smallest average squared error under its prior (section 4). At finite $n$ neither unbiasedness nor maximum likelihood is optimal, and biased estimators can win (section 5).
**What comes out?** Cross-entropy and squared error are negative log-likelihoods, and $L^2$ and $L^1$ penalties are negative log-priors (sections 3 and 4). The mean squared error splits into bias squared plus variance (section 5). Two experiments: three estimators of a variance and Stein's paradox; a loss matched to the noise, and ridge regression as a MAP estimate.
:::

## 1. The central limit theorem

:::thm Theorem | Central limit theorem (Lindeberg–Lévy)
Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. real random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 \in (0, \infty)$, and $\bar X_n = \frac1n\sum_{i\le n} X_i$. Then
$$Z_n = \frac{\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{\ d\ } N(0, 1),$$
that is, $P(Z_n \le z) \to \Phi(z)$ for every $z$.
:::proof Proof sketch
Let $\varphi$ be the characteristic function of $Y = (X_1 - \mu)/\sigma$. Since $\mathbb{E}Y = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}Y^2 = 1$, $\varphi(t) = 1 - t^2/2 + o(t^2)$. The characteristic function of $Z_n = n^{-1/2}\sum_i Y_i$ is $\varphi(t/\sqrt n)^n = \bigl(1 - \frac{t^2}{2n} + o(\frac1n)\bigr)^n \to e^{-t^2/2}$, the characteristic function of $N(0,1)$. Lévy's continuity theorem turns convergence of characteristic functions into convergence in distribution.
:::

:::fig clt | The density of the standardized sum (S − n)/√n of n independent exponential variables of mean 1. For n = 1 it is the skewed exponential density itself; by n = 30 it is close to the normal density (shaded), though the skew is still visible in the tails.

How fast? The Berry–Esseen theorem: if $\rho = \mathbb{E}\lvert X - \mu\rvert^3 < \infty$, then $\sup_z \lvert P(Z_n \le z) - \Phi(z)\rvert \le C\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)$ with an absolute constant $C < 0.48$ (Shevtsova 2011). The theorem needs a finite variance. Without one it fails outright: the mean of $n$ standard Cauchy variables is again standard Cauchy, since its characteristic function is $(e^{-\lvert t\rvert/n})^n = e^{-\lvert t\rvert}$, so averaging gains nothing.

What it buys in practice is the error bar. An average of $n$ draws is off by about $\sigma/\sqrt n$, so halving the error costs four times the data. An accuracy of 0.92 measured on 2,000 test examples has standard error $\sqrt{0.92 \cdot 0.08/2000} \approx 0.006$ (third check question). A mini-batch gradient averages per-example gradients, so its noise shrinks like $1/\sqrt B$ in the batch size $B$, and is often modelled as Gaussian. Whether that model is right is disputed.

:::thm Contested | Is the noise in stochastic gradients Gaussian?
The central limit theorem suggests that a mini-batch gradient is the full gradient plus approximately Gaussian noise, and much analysis of SGD as a stochastic differential equation assumes this. Simsekli, Sagun and Gürbüzbalaban (2019) measured heavy-tailed gradient noise in deep networks and proposed $\alpha$-stable models instead, for which the central limit theorem with finite variance does not apply. Panigrahi et al. (2019) found the noise close to Gaussian for large batches and clearly non-Gaussian for small ones. The answer appears to depend on batch size, architecture and how the tails are measured.
:::

## 2. Estimators and maximum likelihood

:::thm Definition | Model, estimator, likelihood
A *statistical model* is a family of distributions $\{p_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$. Data $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are assumed i.i.d. from some $p_{\theta_0}$. An *estimator* is any function $\hat\theta(x_1, \dots, x_n)$. The *likelihood* is $L(\theta) = \prod_i p_\theta(x_i)$, and a *maximum-likelihood estimator* maximizes it, equivalently minimizes the negative log-likelihood $-\sum_i \ln p_\theta(x_i)$.
:::

For coin flips, $p_\theta(1) = \theta$, the maximum-likelihood estimator is the frequency $k/n$. For $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ it is $\hat\mu = \bar x$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$, which divides by $n$, not $n - 1$; the first experiment compares the two. Week 2 gave the information-theoretic reading: the average negative log-likelihood is $H(\hat p) + D(\hat p\,\|\,p_\theta)$, so maximum likelihood minimizes the KL divergence from the empirical distribution $\hat p$ to the model.

How good can an estimator be? The *score* $s_\theta(x) = \partial_\theta \ln p_\theta(x)$ measures how sensitive the likelihood is to the parameter, and the *Fisher information* $I(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[s_\theta(X)^2]$ measures how much one observation tells about $\theta$.

:::thm Theorem | Cramér–Rao bound
Let $\theta$ be real, let the model be regular enough to differentiate under the integral sign, and let $T(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ be an unbiased estimator: $\mathbb{E}_\theta T = \theta$ for all $\theta$. Then $\operatorname{Var}_\theta T \ge \dfrac{1}{n I(\theta)}$.
:::proof Proof
Let $S = \sum_i s_\theta(x_i)$ be the score of the whole sample. Differentiating $\int p_\theta = 1$ gives $\mathbb{E}_\theta S = 0$, and independence gives $\operatorname{Var}_\theta S = nI(\theta)$. Differentiating $\mathbb{E}_\theta T = \int T \prod_i p_\theta(x_i) = \theta$ gives $\mathbb{E}_\theta[TS] = 1$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(T, S) = 1$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $1 = \operatorname{Cov}(T,S)^2 \le \operatorname{Var}(T)\operatorname{Var}(S) = \operatorname{Var}(T)\,nI(\theta)$.
:::

:::thm Theorem | Asymptotics of maximum likelihood (van der Vaart, chapter 5)
Under regularity conditions (identifiability, smoothness in $\theta$, $I(\theta_0)$ invertible, $\theta_0$ interior), the maximum-likelihood estimator is consistent, and
$$\sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{\ d\ } N\bigl(0, I(\theta_0)^{-1}\bigr).$$
:::proof Proof sketch
The estimator solves $\sum_i s_{\hat\theta}(x_i) = 0$. Expand around $\theta_0$: $0 \approx \sum_i s_{\theta_0}(x_i) + (\hat\theta - \theta_0)\sum_i \partial_\theta s_{\theta_0}(x_i)$. By the central limit theorem $n^{-1/2}\sum_i s_{\theta_0}(x_i) \to N(0, I(\theta_0))$, and by the law of large numbers $\frac1n\sum_i \partial_\theta s_{\theta_0}(x_i) \to -I(\theta_0)$. Solve for $\sqrt n(\hat\theta - \theta_0)$. Making the "$\approx$" rigorous is where the regularity conditions are spent.
:::

So asymptotically the maximum-likelihood estimator attains the Cramér–Rao bound. The bound also shows the price of a wrong model. For a location parameter, the sample median has asymptotic variance $1/(4f(0)^2 n)$, where $f$ is the noise density, against $\sigma^2/n$ for the sample mean (van der Vaart, chapter 21). For Gaussian noise of variance 1 the ratio median to mean is $\pi/2$; for Laplace noise of variance 1, $f(0) = 1/\sqrt 2$ and the ratio is $1/2$. The same holds for regression slopes fitted by least absolute deviations against least squares, which the second experiment measures.

## 3. Losses are negative log-likelihoods

A network for classification outputs a distribution $q_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ over classes; for regression, a prediction $f_\theta(x)$. Both are conditional models, and maximum likelihood applies to the conditional distribution of $y$ given $x$.

:::thm Proposition | Cross-entropy and squared error are maximum likelihood
1. For a classifier, $-\ln \prod_i q_\theta(y_i\mid x_i) = \sum_i -\ln q_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)$, which is $n$ times the cross-entropy loss. Minimizing the loss is maximum likelihood.
2. If $y = f_\theta(x) + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$, the negative log-likelihood is
$$\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i \bigl(y_i - f_\theta(x_i)\bigr)^2 + \frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2).$$
For every $\sigma$ it is minimized in $\theta$ by least squares, and then in $\sigma^2$ by the mean squared residual.
3. If instead $\varepsilon$ has the Laplace density $\frac{1}{2b}e^{-\lvert\varepsilon\rvert/b}$, the negative log-likelihood is $\frac1b\sum_i \lvert y_i - f_\theta(x_i)\rvert + n\ln(2b)$: maximum likelihood is least absolute deviations.
:::proof Proof
Take $-\ln$ of the product of the densities and separate the terms that depend on $\theta$.
:::

:::fig nll | The two losses as negative log-densities of noise with variance 1: the Gaussian gives a parabola, the Laplace density a V. Far from 0 the parabola grows much faster, so least squares lets a single outlier pull the fit; near 0 the V is steeper, so least absolute deviations cares more about getting small residuals exactly to 0.

The choice of loss also decides what is predicted.

:::thm Proposition | Squared error predicts the mean, absolute error the median
For a real random variable $Y$ with finite mean, $\mathbb{E}(Y - c)^2$ is minimized over $c$ by $c = \mathbb{E}Y$, and $\mathbb{E}\lvert Y - c\rvert$ is minimized by any median of $Y$.
:::proof Proof
$\mathbb{E}(Y-c)^2 = \operatorname{Var}Y + (\mathbb{E}Y - c)^2$. For the second, the derivative of $c \mapsto \mathbb{E}\lvert Y - c\rvert$ is $P(Y < c) - P(Y > c)$ where it exists; it is negative below the median and positive above.
:::

So a network trained with squared error estimates $\mathbb{E}[y\mid x]$, and one trained with absolute error estimates the conditional median. Neither is "the" right loss: each is the maximum-likelihood choice for one noise model, and a robust choice for the other's outliers.

:::thm Heuristic | A loss is a noise model
Choosing a loss amounts to choosing what the residuals look like. Squared error is right when errors are many small independent effects, which is the central limit theorem's situation; absolute error, or the Huber loss that interpolates between them, when occasional large errors are expected. The cross-entropy loss assumes nothing beyond a categorical distribution, which is why it is the default for classification.
:::

## 4. Bayesian inference: priors are penalties

:::thm Definition | Prior, posterior, MAP
A *prior* is a distribution $\pi$ on $\Theta$. Bayes' rule gives the *posterior* $p(\theta\mid D) \propto p(D\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)$. The *MAP estimate* maximizes the posterior density; the *posterior mean* is $\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid D]$; the *posterior predictive* is $p(y\mid x, D) = \int p(y\mid x,\theta)\,p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta$.
:::

Taking $-\ln$, the MAP estimate minimizes the negative log-likelihood plus $-\ln \pi(\theta)$: the prior becomes an additive penalty.

:::thm Proposition | Gaussian prior is weight decay, Laplace prior is L1
Take the regression model of section 3 with Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$.
1. With the prior $\theta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)$, the MAP estimate minimizes $\sum_i (y_i - f_\theta(x_i))^2 + \lambda\lvert\theta\rvert^2$ with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.
2. With independent Laplace priors $\pi(\theta_j) \propto e^{-\lvert\theta_j\rvert/b}$, it minimizes $\sum_i (y_i - f_\theta(x_i))^2 + \lambda\lVert\theta\rVert_1$ with $\lambda = 2\sigma^2/b$.
:::proof Proof
The negative log-posterior is, up to a constant, $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i - f_\theta(x_i))^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lvert\theta\rvert^2$ in the first case and $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i - f_\theta(x_i))^2 + \frac1b\lVert\theta\rVert_1$ in the second. Multiply by $2\sigma^2$.
:::

A tighter prior (smaller $\tau$) means a larger penalty, and more noise (larger $\sigma$) means the prior counts for more. For a linear model $f_\theta(x) = x^\top\theta$ the Gaussian case can be solved exactly.

:::thm Proposition | The linear-Gaussian posterior
With $y = X\theta + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)$ and $\theta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)$, the posterior is Gaussian with
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\theta\mid y] &= (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y, \\ \operatorname{Cov}[\theta\mid y] &= \sigma^2(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}, \end{aligned}$$
where $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$. The posterior mean equals the MAP estimate: it is ridge regression.
:::proof Proof
The negative log-posterior is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\bigl(\lvert y - X\theta\rvert^2 + \lambda\lvert\theta\rvert^2\bigr)$ plus a constant, a quadratic in $\theta$ with Hessian $\sigma^{-2}(X^\top X + \lambda I)$ and minimum at the stated mean. A density proportional to $e^{-\text{quadratic}}$ is Gaussian with that mean and the inverse Hessian as covariance.
:::

:::thm Proposition | The posterior mean minimizes average squared error
For any estimator $\delta(D)$, with $\theta$ drawn from the prior and $D$ from $p(D\mid\theta)$,
$$\mathbb{E}\,\lvert\delta(D) - \theta\rvert^2 \ge \mathbb{E}\,\bigl\lvert\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid D] - \theta\bigr\rvert^2.$$
:::proof Proof
Condition on $D$ and write $m = \mathbb{E}[\theta\mid D]$. Then $\mathbb{E}[\lvert\delta - \theta\rvert^2\mid D] = \lvert\delta - m\rvert^2 + \mathbb{E}[\lvert m - \theta\rvert^2\mid D]$, because the cross term $2(\delta - m)\cdot\mathbb{E}[m - \theta\mid D]$ vanishes. Take expectations over $D$.
:::

The second experiment checks this on simulated data: with the true prior, ridge at $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ beats least squares by a wide margin when there are barely more observations than coefficients. The figure at the top showed the difference between the two penalties. In one dimension it is explicit.

:::thm Proposition | Soft thresholding
For $z \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\lambda > 0$:
$$\begin{aligned} &\arg\min_w\ \tfrac12(w - z)^2 + \lambda\lvert w\rvert \\ &\qquad = \operatorname{sign}(z)\max(\lvert z\rvert - \lambda, 0), \\ &\arg\min_w\ \tfrac12(w - z)^2 + \tfrac\lambda2 w^2 \\ &\qquad = \frac{z}{1 + \lambda}. \end{aligned}$$
The $L^1$ solution is exactly 0 whenever $\lvert z\rvert \le \lambda$; the $L^2$ solution is 0 only if $z = 0$.
:::proof Proof
The first objective is convex. Its subdifferential at $w \ne 0$ is $w - z + \lambda\operatorname{sign}(w)$, and at $w = 0$ it is the interval $[-z - \lambda, -z + \lambda]$, which contains 0 exactly when $\lvert z\rvert \le \lambda$. Solving $w - z + \lambda\operatorname{sign}(w) = 0$ otherwise gives the formula. The second objective is a smooth quadratic with derivative $(1 + \lambda)w - z$.
:::

This is the lasso (Tibshirani 1996). In the figure, the corners of the diamond are where the subdifferential is large enough to hold the minimum.

As data accumulate, the prior matters less. Under regularity conditions the posterior is asymptotically $N\bigl(\hat\theta_n, (nI(\theta_0))^{-1}\bigr)$, centred at the maximum-likelihood estimate (the Bernstein–von Mises theorem; van der Vaart, chapter 10). So for a fixed model and growing data, Bayesian and maximum-likelihood answers agree. A network has more parameters than data points, which is where they need not agree.

:::thm Contested | Is the Bayes posterior the right target for deep networks?
Wenzel et al. (2020) found that for the networks and priors they studied, tempering the posterior to $p(\theta\mid D)^{1/T}$ with $T < 1$, a "cold posterior", predicted better than the Bayes posterior itself, $T = 1$. If the Bayesian model were right, $T = 1$ should be best. Proposed explanations include misspecified priors (Fortuin et al. 2022) and data augmentation and curated datasets, which change the effective likelihood (Aitchison 2021). With careful sampling and no augmentation, Izmailov et al. (2021) found the effect largely disappears. Whether Bayesian neural networks are worth their cost in practice remains debated.
:::

## 5. Evaluating estimators: bias, variance, MSE and MAE

:::thm Definition | Bias, variance, mean squared error
For an estimator $\hat\theta$ of $\theta$: $\operatorname{bias} = \mathbb{E}\hat\theta - \theta$, $\operatorname{MSE} = \mathbb{E}\lvert\hat\theta - \theta\rvert^2$, $\operatorname{MAE} = \mathbb{E}\lvert\hat\theta - \theta\rvert$.
:::

:::thm Proposition | Bias–variance decomposition
$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \lvert\operatorname{bias}\rvert^2 + \mathbb{E}\lvert\hat\theta - \mathbb{E}\hat\theta\rvert^2$.
:::proof Proof
Write $\hat\theta - \theta = (\hat\theta - \mathbb{E}\hat\theta) + (\mathbb{E}\hat\theta - \theta)$ and expand the square; the cross term has mean 0.
:::

An unbiased estimator spends nothing on the first term, which can force it to spend a lot on the second. The classic example is the variance.

:::thm Proposition | The best divisor for a Gaussian variance is n + 1
Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be i.i.d. $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $S = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$. Among the estimators $S/d$,
$$\operatorname{MSE}(S/d) = \sigma^4\,\frac{(n - 1 - d)^2 + 2(n - 1)}{d^2},$$
which is minimized at $d = n + 1$. So $S/(n+1)$ beats both the unbiased $S/(n-1)$ and the maximum-likelihood $S/n$.
:::proof Proof
$S/\sigma^2$ has the $\chi^2$ distribution with $k = n - 1$ degrees of freedom: mean $k$, variance $2k$. So $S/d$ has bias $\sigma^2(k/d - 1)$ and variance $2k\sigma^4/d^2$, and the decomposition gives the formula. As a function of $u = 1/d$ it is $\sigma^4\bigl((k^2 + 2k)u^2 - 2ku + 1\bigr)$, minimized at $u = 1/(k + 2) = 1/(n+1)$.
:::

The gap is small for the variance. In several dimensions it can be large, and the result is one of the surprises of twentieth-century statistics.

:::thm Theorem | Stein's paradox (Stein 1956; James and Stein 1961)
Let $X \sim N(\theta, I_d)$ with $d \ge 3$. The James–Stein estimator $\delta(X) = \bigl(1 - \frac{d-2}{\lvert X\rvert^2}\bigr)X$ satisfies
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\,\lvert\delta(X) - \theta\rvert^2 &= d - (d-2)^2\,\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{\lvert X\rvert^2} \\ &< d = \mathbb{E}\,\lvert X - \theta\rvert^2 \end{aligned}$$
for every $\theta$. So the maximum-likelihood estimator $X$ is inadmissible.
:::proof Proof
Stein's lemma: for $X \sim N(\theta, I_d)$ and weakly differentiable $g\colon\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ with enough integrability, $\mathbb{E}[(X - \theta)\cdot g(X)] = \mathbb{E}[\nabla\cdot g(X)]$, by integrating by parts against the Gaussian density. Take $g(x) = x/\lvert x\rvert^2$, whose divergence is $(d - 2)/\lvert x\rvert^2$; for $d \ge 3$ the singularity at 0 is integrable. Then
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\lvert\delta - \theta\rvert^2 &= \mathbb{E}\lvert X - \theta\rvert^2 \\ &\quad - 2(d-2)\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X - \theta)\cdot g(X)\bigr] \\ &\quad + (d-2)^2\,\mathbb{E}\lvert X\rvert^{-2} \\ &= d - (d - 2)^2\,\mathbb{E}\lvert X\rvert^{-2}. \end{aligned}$$
:::

The coordinates can be entirely unrelated, the batting averages of baseball players and the price of tea, and the joint estimator still wins in total squared error, though not necessarily for each coordinate. The empirical-Bayes reading: if the $\theta_j$ were drawn from $N(0, \tau^2)$, the posterior mean would be $\frac{\tau^2}{1+\tau^2}X$, and $1 - (d-2)/\lvert X\rvert^2$ is an estimate of $\frac{\tau^2}{1 + \tau^2}$ from the data.

The mean absolute error weighs errors linearly rather than quadratically, so it is less dominated by rare large errors, and by section 3 it is minimized by medians rather than means. Which to report is a choice about what errors cost; root mean squared error is reported for having the units of the quantity.

:::thm Heuristic | Unbiasedness is not the goal
Bias is one of two terms in the error. Regularization, early stopping, shrinkage and priors all add bias to remove variance, and they help exactly when the variance removed is larger, typically when parameters are many and data are few. Deep learning lives in that regime.
:::

## 6. What carries over to deep networks

The classical results above assume a fixed, finite-dimensional model and $n \to \infty$. A network has more parameters than training examples, the maximum-likelihood estimate is not unique (many weight vectors fit the training set perfectly), and which one gradient descent finds depends on the optimizer and its initialization, which then act as an implicit prior. The losses and penalties carry over; the asymptotic guarantees do not.

:::thm Empirical | Weight decay is not $L^2$ regularization under Adam (Loshchilov and Hutter 2019)
For plain gradient descent, adding $\frac\lambda2\lvert\theta\rvert^2$ to the loss and multiplying the weights by $1 - \eta\lambda$ at each step are the same thing. For adaptive optimizers such as Adam (week 4) they are not: the gradient of the penalty is rescaled by the per-coordinate step sizes, so weights with large gradient histories are decayed less. Loshchilov and Hutter decoupled the decay from the gradient step (AdamW) and found it generalized better on image classification; AdamW is now the default for training transformers.
:::

## Check yourself

:::check
Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be coin flips with $P(x_i = 1) = \theta$. Find the maximum-likelihood estimator, its variance, and the Fisher information. Does it attain the Cramér–Rao bound?
---
The log-likelihood is $k\ln\theta + (n - k)\ln(1 - \theta)$ with $k = \sum_i x_i$, maximized at $\hat\theta = k/n$, which is unbiased with variance $\theta(1-\theta)/n$. The score of one flip is $x/\theta - (1 - x)/(1 - \theta)$, with variance $I(\theta) = \frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)}$. So $1/(nI(\theta)) = \theta(1-\theta)/n$: the bound is attained exactly, for every $n$.
:::

:::check
In one dimension, the lasso estimate with penalty $\lambda = 1$ is the soft threshold of $z$. What is it for $z = 0.5$, $z = 1.5$ and $z = -3$, and what would ridge with the same $\lambda$ give?
---
Soft thresholding gives $0$, $0.5$ and $-2$: inputs within $\lambda$ of 0 are set to 0, the others moved $\lambda$ towards 0. Ridge, $\arg\min \frac12(w - z)^2 + \frac12 w^2$, gives $z/2$: $0.25$, $0.75$ and $-1.5$. It shrinks proportionally and never reaches 0.
:::

:::check
A classifier has accuracy 0.92 on 2,000 test examples, and a second has 0.93 on a different 2,000. Is the second better?
---
Each accuracy is an average of 2,000 indicators, so by the central limit theorem its standard error is about $\sqrt{0.92 \cdot 0.08/2000} \approx 0.0061$. The difference of two independent estimates has standard error about $\sqrt2 \times 0.0061 \approx 0.0086$, and $0.01/0.0086 \approx 1.2$ standard errors is well within chance. On the same 2,000 examples the comparison would be sharper, since the two models' errors are correlated and the paired difference has smaller variance.
:::
