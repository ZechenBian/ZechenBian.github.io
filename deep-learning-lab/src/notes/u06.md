:::lede
Week 7's transformer reads a whole sequence at once. Before it, sequences were read one element at a time by a recurrent network, which keeps a running summary, its hidden state, and updates it with the same map at every step. This week covers why such networks forget, how the LSTM's additive memory fixes that, and how to write text with any next-token model. Start with one number fed back into itself sixty times.
:::

:::widget bptt

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Model sequences of any length, such as text, speech or time series: read them one element at a time while carrying a summary in a hidden state, and generate them one element at a time.
**Why can it be done this way?** One map applied at every step, with the same weights, handles any length. Training unrolls the recurrence into a deep network with shared weights and backpropagates through time (sections 1 and 2).
**Why should it be done this way?** Sharing weights across time is to sequences what convolution is to images: the same rule applies at every position. The gradient analysis then explains why the plain recurrence forgets and why an additive memory does not (sections 2 and 3).
**What comes out?** LSTMs ran machine translation and speech recognition until transformers took over (section 5). The decoding rules, greedy, beam search, temperature and nucleus sampling, apply to any next-token model, including the language models of weeks 7 and 8 (section 4). Two experiments: a plain and an additive memory over 60 steps of noise, and five decoders on one model of Shakespeare.
:::

## 1. Recurrent networks

:::thm Definition | Recurrent neural network
Given inputs $x_1, \dots, x_T \in \mathbb{R}^d$, a (plain, or Elman) recurrent network computes hidden states $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and outputs $y_t$ by
$$\begin{aligned} h_t &= \sigma(Wx_t + Uh_{t-1} + b), \quad h_0 = 0, \\ y_t &= g(Vh_t + c), \end{aligned}$$
with the same $W, U, b, V, c$ at every step, typically $\sigma = \tanh$ and $g$ a softmax. A loss is summed over the steps that have targets: at every step for language modelling, at the last step for classifying a whole sequence.
:::

:::fig unroll | A recurrent network is a loop. Unrolled over the length of the input, it is a deep feedforward network whose layers all share the same weights; training backpropagates through that unrolled network, one Jacobian per step (blue).

The hidden state is the network's memory: everything about $x_1, \dots, x_t$ that influences later outputs has to pass through the $m$ numbers of $h_t$. Expressively, nothing is missing: recurrent networks with rational weights and a saturated-linear activation can simulate any Turing machine in real time (Siegelmann and Sontag 1995). That theorem uses unbounded precision in the hidden state, which trained networks do not have. The practical question is what gradient descent can find.

## 2. Backpropagation through time and vanishing gradients

:::thm Proposition | The gradient through time
Let $\ell_t$ be the loss at step $t$ and $a_k = Wx_k + Uh_{k-1} + b$. Then
$$\frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial U} = \sum_{s=1}^{t} \delta_s\,h_{s-1}^\top, \qquad \delta_s^\top = \frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial h_t}\Bigl(\prod_{k=t}^{s+1} J_k\Bigr)D_s,$$
where $D_k = \operatorname{diag}\sigma'(a_k)$ and $J_k = \dfrac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} = D_kU$, with the product ordered from $k = t$ on the left down to $k = s + 1$ on the right.
:::proof Proof
$U$ enters $h_t$ at every step $s \le t$, through $a_s$. By the chain rule, $\frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial U} = \sum_s \frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial a_s}\frac{\partial a_s}{\partial U}$, where the second factor contributes $h_{s-1}^\top$, and $\frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial a_s} = \frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial h_t}\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}\cdots\frac{\partial h_{s+1}}{\partial h_s}\frac{\partial h_s}{\partial a_s}$. Each $\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} = D_kU$ and $\frac{\partial h_s}{\partial a_s} = D_s$.
:::

The contribution of step $s$ to the gradient is weighted by a product of $t - s$ Jacobians. That product decides whether the network can learn that an input at step $s$ matters for the output at step $t$.

:::thm Proposition | Vanishing and exploding gradients
If $\lvert\sigma'\rvert \le \gamma$ (for tanh, $\gamma = 1$), then $\bigl\lVert\prod_{k=t}^{s+1}J_k\bigr\rVert \le (\gamma\lVert U\rVert)^{t-s}$. So if the largest singular value of $U$ is below $1/\gamma$, the contribution of step $s$ to the gradient at step $t$ decays exponentially in $t - s$. Conversely, a large singular value is necessary for gradients to explode.
:::proof Proof
Submultiplicativity: $\lVert D_kU\rVert \le \lVert D_k\rVert\lVert U\rVert \le \gamma\lVert U\rVert$. If $\gamma\lVert U\rVert < 1$ the product norm is at most $(\gamma\lVert U\rVert)^{t-s} \to 0$; if the product grows without bound, then $\gamma\lVert U\rVert > 1$.
:::

The converse direction fails: $\lVert U\rVert > 1$ does not prevent vanishing, because saturated units have $\sigma' \approx 0$. The figure at the top shows both ways of losing the gradient in one dimension. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (1994) made the dilemma precise: for a network to store a bit robustly against small perturbations, its dynamics must contract near the stored state, and contraction is exactly what makes the gradient from far back vanish (Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio 2013 give a modern account). So the plain recurrence can learn long dependencies only through a fragile balance. Exploding gradients are the easier problem: rescale the gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold (*gradient clipping*), a standard trick ever since.

:::thm Heuristic | Long dependencies are learned last, or not at all
Even when gradients do not vanish completely, contributions from far back are exponentially smaller than those from nearby steps. Gradient descent then fits the short-range structure first, and the long-range signal is buried under it. Adaptive optimizers rescale each parameter's step by the size of its own gradient, which partly compensates; the first experiment shows a plain RNN sometimes, but not reliably, learning a delay of 60 steps this way.
:::

## 3. Gated cells: LSTM and GRU

:::thm Definition | Long short-term memory (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997; forget gate: Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins 2000)
With $z_t = (x_t, h_{t-1})$, the sigmoid $\varsigma$ and elementwise products $\odot$:
$$\begin{aligned} f_t &= \varsigma(W_fz_t + b_f), \quad i_t = \varsigma(W_iz_t + b_i), \\ o_t &= \varsigma(W_oz_t + b_o), \quad g_t = \tanh(W_gz_t + b_g), \\ c_t &= f_t\odot c_{t-1} + i_t\odot g_t, \\ h_t &= o_t\odot\tanh(c_t). \end{aligned}$$
The *cell state* $c_t$ is written to additively; the gates $f, i, o \in (0,1)^m$ decide what to keep, what to add and what to show.
:::

:::thm Proposition | The cell state carries the gradient
$\dfrac{\partial c_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} = \operatorname{diag}(f_t) + R_t$, where $R_t$ collects the terms that pass through $h_{t-1}$ (and so through the gates and $g_t$). Along the direct path, the gradient from $c_t$ back to $c_s$ is multiplied by $\prod_{k=s+1}^{t}\operatorname{diag}(f_k)$, so it survives as long as the forget gates stay near 1. With no forget gate ($f_t \equiv 1$), the direct path is the identity: the *constant error carousel*.
:::proof Proof
Differentiate $c_t = f_t\odot c_{t-1} + i_t\odot g_t$ with respect to $c_{t-1}$: the explicit dependence gives $\operatorname{diag}(f_t)$, and $f_t$, $i_t$, $g_t$ depend on $c_{t-1}$ only through $h_{t-1} = o_{t-1}\odot\tanh(c_{t-1})$, which gives $R_t$. Multiplying the direct terms over $k$ gives the product of the diagonal matrices.
:::

The contrast with section 2 is the point: the plain recurrence multiplies by $D_kU$, a matrix the network cannot keep near the identity without giving up on representing anything, while the cell multiplies by a diagonal of gate values that the network can set to 1 for whatever it wants to remember, independently of what it computes. The second experiment uses the cell without its forget gate; the gradient from the end reaches the first step undiminished.

:::thm Empirical | What matters in an LSTM
Jozefowicz, Zaremba and Sutskever (2015) evaluated thousands of recurrent architectures found by search. None was clearly better than the LSTM across tasks, and the most important single detail was to initialize the forget-gate bias to about 1, so that the cell remembers by default. Greff et al. (2017) found the forget gate and the output activation the most critical components.
:::

The gated recurrent unit (GRU, Cho et al. 2014) merges the forget and input gates into one *update* gate $u_t$ and drops the separate cell: $h_t = (1 - u_t)\odot h_{t-1} + u_t\odot\tilde h_t$. It is the same idea, an interpolation that can copy the past forward unchanged, with fewer parameters.

## 4. Writing with a model: greedy, beam search, temperature, nucleus

A model of $p(x_t\mid x_{<t})$ defines a distribution over whole sequences, $p(x_{1:n}) = \prod_t p(x_t\mid x_{<t})$. Writing with it means choosing a decoding rule. Each one optimizes something different.

:::thm Definition | Decoding rules
1. *Greedy*: $x_t = \arg\max_a p(a\mid x_{<t})$.
2. *Beam search* with width $B$: keep the $B$ prefixes of highest probability; at each step extend each by every token and keep the best $B$ of the results. It approximates the most probable sequence, $\arg\max_{x_{1:n}} p(x_{1:n})$.
3. *Sampling at temperature* $T$: if the model's logits are $z$, draw $x_t$ from $p_T(a) \propto e^{z_a/T}$.
4. *Nucleus (top-$p$) sampling* (Holtzman et al. 2020): draw from $p$ restricted to the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to at least $p$, renormalized. *Top-$k$*: restricted to the $k$ most probable tokens.
:::

:::fig beamtree | Greedy decoding is not the same as finding the most probable sequence. Greedy takes A, the likelier first token, but every continuation of A is mediocre: the best sequence it can reach has probability 0.2. A beam of width 2 keeps B as well and finds Bx with probability 0.36.

Exact maximization is intractable in general, since the number of sequences grows like $\lvert\mathcal V\rvert^n$, and beam search is a heuristic for it. The temperature has a precise variational meaning.

:::thm Proposition | What temperature optimizes
For logits $z \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal V}$ and $T > 0$, the distribution $p_T \propto e^{z/T}$ is the unique maximizer over all distributions $q$ of
$$\mathbb{E}_q[z] + T\,H(q),$$
the expected logit plus $T$ times the entropy (in nats). As $T \to 0$, $p_T$ concentrates on $\arg\max z$ (greedy); as $T \to \infty$ it tends to the uniform distribution; and $H(p_T)$ is nondecreasing in $T$.
:::proof Proof
Let $Z = \sum_a e^{z_a/T}$. For any $q$, $\mathbb{E}_q[z] + T H(q) = T\bigl(\ln Z - D(q\,\|\,p_T)\bigr)$, since $\ln p_T(a) = z_a/T - \ln Z$. By Gibbs' inequality (week 2) this is maximized exactly at $q = p_T$. The limits follow from the formula for $p_T$. With $\beta = 1/T$, a direct computation gives $\frac{dH(p_T)}{d\beta} = -\beta\operatorname{Var}_{p_T}(z) \le 0$.
:::

So temperature trades score against diversity, with $T = 1$ reproducing the model. Beam search goes to the other extreme, maximizing probability with no entropy at all. That is wrong for open-ended text, for a reason from week 2: real text is *typical*, not *probable*.

:::thm Heuristic | Typical text is not the most probable text
For a stationary ergodic source, the Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem says that $-\frac1n\log_2 p(X_{1:n}) \to \mathcal H$, the entropy rate, with probability 1: long samples have probability about $2^{-n\mathcal H}$ (Cover and Thomas, chapter 16). The most probable sequences have much higher probability and are not typical: they are repetitive and bland. If a language model is a good model of text, its typical samples resemble text and its mode does not. Holtzman et al. (2020) documented this "degeneration" in neural language models, and the second experiment shows it in a model of Shakespeare: beam search finds text far more probable than Shakespeare's own, and repetitive.
:::

:::thm Empirical | The beam search curse in translation
In machine translation, where the output is nearly determined by the input, beam search with a small beam works well, but larger beams often make translations worse (Koehn and Knowles 2017). Searching exactly for the most probable translation, Stahlberg and Byrne (2019) found that for more than half the sentences it is the empty translation: the model assigns the end-of-sentence token at the first step more probability than any full translation. Short outputs win because every extra token multiplies the probability by a number below 1; beam search is usually run with a length normalization for this reason.
:::

:::thm Contested | Is maximizing probability the wrong goal, or a symptom of model error?
One view (Holtzman et al. 2020) is that human text deliberately avoids the most probable continuations, so decoding should sample from a truncated distribution, and that maximizing probability is the wrong objective for open-ended generation. Another (Meister, Cotterell and Vieira 2020) is that beam search succeeds in translation because it implicitly favours text whose information is spread evenly, a property of human language, and that the empty-string and repetition problems show that models put probability in the wrong places. On that view, better models would make the mode better. Both are consistent with the data; which matters more is open.
:::

## 5. Encoder–decoder models and case studies

*Representation learning* is the idea that the hidden states of a network trained on one task become useful descriptions of the input. For sequence classification, such as the sentiment of a review, a recurrent network reads the text and a linear layer classifies the last hidden state $h_T$, which is trained to be a summary of the whole sequence with respect to the task. For tasks whose output is itself a sequence, two recurrent networks are composed.

:::thm Definition | Encoder–decoder (sequence to sequence)
An *encoder* RNN reads the source $x_{1:S}$ into a final state $h_S$. A *decoder* RNN, started from $h_S$, models the target autoregressively, $p(y_t\mid y_{<t}, x) $, feeding its own previous output back as input. It is trained on pairs by the cross-entropy of the target tokens given the source and the correct previous tokens (*teacher forcing*), and it writes translations with one of the decoding rules of section 4.
:::

:::fig encdec | An encoder–decoder for translation. Everything the decoder knows about the source passes through one vector, the encoder's last state.

:::thm Empirical | Neural machine translation (Sutskever, Vinyals and Le 2014; Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio 2015; Wu et al. 2016)
Sutskever et al. trained deep LSTM encoder–decoders on English–French (WMT'14) and reached a BLEU score of 34.8 with an ensemble, competitive with phrase-based systems; reversing the order of the source sentence helped markedly, because it put the first source words close to the first target words. Bahdanau et al. removed the single-vector bottleneck by letting the decoder look at all encoder states through learned weights at each step, *attention*, which made long sentences translatable and is the mechanism week 7's transformer is built from. Google's production translation system moved to LSTM encoder–decoders with attention in 2016.
:::

What do hidden states learn? In character-level LSTMs trained on text, Karpathy, Johnson and Fei-Fei (2015) found individual cells that track interpretable properties, such as whether the current position is inside a quotation or how deep the nesting of brackets is in source code, along with many cells with no clear interpretation.

:::check
For the one-number network $h_t = \tanh(wh_{t-1} + x_t)$, write $\partial h_T/\partial h_0$. Bound it for $w = 0.9$ and $T = 60$. What happens for $w = 1$ if all inputs and $h_0$ are 0?
---
$\partial h_T/\partial h_0 = \prod_{t=1}^{T} w\bigl(1 - h_t^2\bigr)$, so its absolute value is at most $\lvert w\rvert^T = 0.9^{60} \approx 0.0018$, and less wherever $h_t \ne 0$. With $w = 1$ and zero inputs, $h_t = 0$ for all $t$ and every factor is exactly 1: the gradient survives. Any input that moves $h$ away from 0 makes the factors smaller than 1, which is why the figure shows survival only at $w \approx 1$ with small inputs.
:::

:::check
A model's logits for three tokens are $(2, 1, 0)$. Compute the sampling distribution at temperature 1 and at temperature 0.5, and the set kept by nucleus sampling with $p = 0.9$ at temperature 1.
---
At $T = 1$: $e^{(2,1,0)}/\sum \approx (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$. At $T = 0.5$ the logits become $(4, 2, 0)$, giving $\approx(0.867, 0.117, 0.016)$: sharper. The two largest probabilities at $T = 1$ add up to $0.910 \ge 0.9$, so nucleus sampling keeps the first two tokens and renormalizes to $(0.731, 0.269)$.
:::

:::check
In the figure of section 4, what is the probability of the sequence found by greedy decoding, and of the one found by a beam of width 2? Can a beam of width $B$ ever return a sequence less probable than greedy's?
---
Greedy finds $Ax$ with $0.5 \cdot 0.4 = 0.2$; the beam finds $Bx$ with $0.4 \cdot 0.9 = 0.36$. Yes, it can: beams of width 1 and $B$ follow different prefixes, and the greedy prefix can drop out of a wider beam if, say, $B$ other prefixes look better at an early step and all later turn out worse. Beam search is not monotone in the width, which is related to the curse in the Empirical box above.
