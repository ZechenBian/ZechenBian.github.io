Week 12 learned values and acted greedily on them. This week differentiates the expected return with respect to the parameters of the policy itself. The gradient has a simple form that needs no model and no value function, only a way to sample; subtracting a baseline makes it usable, and clipping the step makes it reusable. The result, PPO, is what the course's Colab task applies to Blackjack, and the same idea, with a checker in place of a reward, trains today's reasoning models. Start with the clip, on one sample.

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Find a policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ with the largest expected return $J(\theta)$ by gradient ascent on $\theta$, for problems where acting greedily on values is awkward: continuous actions, policies that must be random, and language models whose "actions" are tokens.
**Why can it be done this way?** The log-derivative trick turns $\nabla J$ into an expectation over trajectories of the policy itself, which can be estimated from samples without knowing the environment (section 2). A baseline removes much of the variance without adding bias, and a critic supplies it (section 3).
**Why should it be done this way?** The objective is the return itself, not a proxy, and small changes to $\theta$ change the policy smoothly, unlike an argmax. The performance difference lemma says how far a new policy may move from the one that collected the data before the estimate stops being trustworthy; PPO's clip enforces that cheaply (section 4).
**What comes out?** Stochastic policies where they are needed, as in rock, paper, scissors (section 5), and the reinforcement learning stage of reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1, next to the search-based systems AlphaGeometry and AlphaCode (section 6). Two experiments: PPO against the plain policy gradient on Blackjack, measured against week 12's exact optimum, and two policy-gradient learners playing rock, paper, scissors.
:::

## 1. Why optimize the policy directly

Value-based control acts by $\arg\max_a Q(s, a)$. That has three limitations. With continuous actions, the argmax is itself an optimization problem at every step. A tiny change in $Q$ can flip the argmax and change the policy abruptly. And the greedy policy is deterministic, while some problems need randomness: against an opponent who adapts, any deterministic choice in rock, paper, scissors loses every time (section 5), and when the state does not reveal everything, randomizing can beat every deterministic policy.

So parametrize the policy directly, for instance by a softmax of scores, $\pi_\theta(a \mid s) \propto e^{h_\theta(s, a)}$, or for a continuous action by a Gaussian whose mean and variance are network outputs; a language model is such a policy over tokens. Maximize
$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\bigl[R(\tau)\bigr], \qquad R(\tau) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_{t+1},$$
where the trajectory $\tau = (s_0, a_0, r_1, s_1, \dots)$ is generated by the policy in the environment.

## 2. The policy gradient theorem

:::thm Theorem | Policy gradient (Williams 1992; Sutton et al. 2000)
$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Bigl[&\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\\ &\times\bigl(G_t - b(s_t)\bigr)\Bigr]\end{aligned}$$
for the reward-to-go $G_t = \sum_{k \ge t}\gamma^{k-t}r_{k+1}$ and any function $b$ of the state alone, the *baseline*. (In practice the factor $\gamma^t$ is usually dropped.)
:::proof Proof
The probability of a trajectory is $p_\theta(\tau) = \rho(s_0)\prod_t\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,p(s_{t+1}, r_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$, and only the policy factors depend on $\theta$. So $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$, and by the log-derivative trick of week 9, $\nabla J = \nabla\int R\,p_\theta = \int R\,p_\theta\nabla\log p_\theta = \mathbb{E}\bigl[R(\tau)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\bigr]$. Two terms vanish in expectation. A reward $r_{k+1}$ with $k < t$ is fixed before $a_t$ is drawn, and $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \mid s_t] = \sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a \mid s_t) = \nabla 1 = 0$; so only the rewards from $t$ on remain, and $\sum_{k \ge t}\gamma^k r_{k+1} = \gamma^t G_t$. The same identity kills $b(s_t)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$.
:::

The estimate from sampled episodes, with $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)(G_t - b(s_t))$, is *REINFORCE*. It is unbiased for any baseline, but its variance depends on the baseline a great deal: a good action in a bad episode still gets a negative return, and only averaging over many episodes sorts this out. The best simple choice is close to $b(s) = v_\pi(s)$, which turns $G_t - b(s_t)$ into an estimate of the *advantage* $A_\pi(s_t, a_t) = q_\pi(s_t, a_t) - v_\pi(s_t)$: positive when the action did better than the policy does on average.

## 3. Actor–critic and the advantage

An *actor–critic* method learns both: an actor $\pi_\theta$ and a critic $\hat v_w \approx v_\pi$, trained by week 12's methods. The critic can replace the return in the advantage. The TD error $\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma\hat v_w(s_{t+1}) - \hat v_w(s_t)$ is an estimate of $A_\pi(s_t, a_t)$, unbiased when $\hat v_w = v_\pi$, with much lower variance than $G_t - \hat v_w(s_t)$ but biased when the critic is wrong: the bias–variance trade of section 4 of week 12 again. *Generalized advantage estimation* (Schulman et al. 2016) interpolates, $\hat A_t = \sum_{l \ge 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}$, with $\lambda = 1$ giving $G_t - \hat v_w(s_t)$ and $\lambda = 0$ the TD error.

In Blackjack, as in the first experiment, all the reward comes at the end and there is no discounting, so $G_t$ is the final result, and the critic is a table of average returns per state.

## 4. Trust regions and PPO

Each batch of experience is collected by the current policy $\pi$; the update produces $\pi'$. How large a step is safe? Write $d_{\pi}(s) = (1 - \gamma)\sum_t\gamma^t\Pr_\pi(s_t = s)$ for the discounted state distribution.

:::thm Lemma | Performance difference (Kakade and Langford 2002)
For any two policies,
$$J(\pi') - J(\pi) = \frac{1}{1 - \gamma}\,\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_{\pi'}}\,\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi'(\cdot \mid s)}\bigl[A_\pi(s, a)\bigr].$$
:::proof Proof
Along a trajectory of $\pi'$ starting from $s_0$, the sum $\sum_t\gamma^t\bigl(r_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(s_{t+1}) - v_\pi(s_t)\bigr)$ telescopes to $R(\tau) - v_\pi(s_0)$. Take expectations under $\pi'$: the left side has expectation $\mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\sum_t\gamma^t A_\pi(s_t, a_t)$, because $\mathbb{E}[r_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(s_{t+1}) \mid s_t, a_t] = q_\pi(s_t, a_t)$; the right side has expectation $J(\pi') - J(\pi)$. Regrouping the sum by states gives the factor $\frac{1}{1-\gamma}$ and $d_{\pi'}$.
:::

The trouble is $d_{\pi'}$: states visited by the new policy, which we have no samples from. Replacing it by $d_\pi$, and the inner expectation by importance weights, gives the *surrogate*
$$L_\pi(\pi') = \frac{1}{1 - \gamma}\,\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_\pi,\ a \sim \pi}\Bigl[\frac{\pi'(a \mid s)}{\pi(a \mid s)}A_\pi(s, a)\Bigr],$$
which can be estimated from the batch, agrees with $J(\pi') - J(\pi)$ to first order at $\pi' = \pi$ (its gradient there is the policy gradient), and is accurate only while $\pi'$ stays close to $\pi$. Schulman et al. (2015) prove $J(\pi') - J(\pi) \ge L_\pi(\pi') - C\max_s\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot \mid s)\,\|\,\pi'(\cdot \mid s)\bigr)$ with $C = 4\gamma\max|A_\pi|/(1 - \gamma)^2$, so improving the surrogate while keeping the KL divergence small improves the policy. TRPO maximizes $L$ under a constraint on the average KL, with a second-order method.

:::thm Definition | Proximal policy optimization (Schulman et al. 2017)
With $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)$ and advantage estimates $\hat A_t$, maximize
$$\begin{aligned}L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Bigl[\min\bigl(&r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\\ &\mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)\,\hat A_t\bigr)\Bigr]\end{aligned}$$
by several epochs of minibatch gradient steps on each batch, typically with $\varepsilon = 0.2$, together with the critic's loss and often an entropy bonus.
:::

The widget shows what the clip does to one sample. Once the ratio has moved by $\varepsilon$ in the direction the advantage favours, the sample's gradient is zero, so reusing the batch cannot push the policy arbitrarily far on its account; in the unfavourable direction nothing is clipped, and the min makes $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ a lower bound on the unclipped surrogate. This is not a trust region in the sense of the theorem, since nothing bounds the KL divergence directly, but it behaves like one in practice, and it needs only first-order steps.

:::thm Contested | What makes PPO work
Engstrom et al. (2020) found that much of PPO's advantage over TRPO in standard benchmarks came from implementation details outside the clipped objective, such as normalizing advantages and observations, clipping the value loss and annealing the learning rate, and that the clip by itself did not keep the ratios within $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$. PPO remains the default for fine-tuning language models from feedback, and simpler variants that drop the critic work well there (section 6); which ingredients matter, and when, is still argued.
:::

## 5. Games: when the policy must be random

:::thm Proposition | Rock, paper, scissors
In the zero-sum game where a win pays $+1$, a loss $-1$ and a tie $0$, the only equilibrium is for both players to play each move with probability $\frac13$, and its value is $0$. Against an opponent who knows the policy, any deterministic policy loses $1$ per round, and a policy $p$ loses $\max(p_R - p_S,\ p_P - p_R,\ p_S - p_P)$, its *exploitability*.
:::proof Proof
Against $p = (p_R, p_P, p_S)$ the opponent's three moves earn $p_R - p_S$ (paper), $p_P - p_R$ (scissors) and $p_S - p_P$ (rock). These sum to $0$, so their maximum is positive unless all three are $0$, that is, unless $p$ is uniform. The same holds for the other player by symmetry.
:::

A value-based learner acting greedily on $Q$ plays a deterministic policy, which is exploitable by $1$. A policy-gradient learner can represent the equilibrium. Whether it finds it is another matter. When both players follow the gradients of their own payoffs, the equilibrium is not an attractor: for the closely related replicator and follow-the-regularized-leader dynamics, continuous-time orbits cycle around it forever (Mertikopoulos, Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2018), and discrete steps spiral slowly outward (Bailey and Piliouras 2018). What does converge is the time average: if both players have no regret, as with multiplicative weights, the average of their policies converges to the equilibrium (Freund and Schapire 1999).

:::fig rpscycle | Player 1's policy as a point in the triangle of mixed strategies, under simultaneous policy gradient for 5,000 steps from a slight lean towards rock. It circles the equilibrium at the centre in loops that never close and slowly widen; the average of the policies it has played sits at the centre.

The second experiment runs these dynamics and measures both. Self-play systems such as AlphaZero (week 12) and the poker programs Libratus and Pluribus face the same issue at scale, and use search, averaging or regret minimization rather than the last policy alone.

## 6. Case study: reasoning models

A language model is a policy: the state is the prompt and the tokens written so far, the action is the next token, and an answer to a problem is an episode. When the answer can be checked automatically, against a known result or by running unit tests, the check is a reward, and the policy gradient can train the model to reason better without anyone writing out the reasoning.

:::fig rlvr | Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. For each problem the model samples a group of answers; a checker scores them; each answer is reinforced in proportion to how much better than the group it did; a clipped objective and a KL penalty keep the model near where it started.

:::thm Empirical | DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI 2025)
DeepSeek-R1-Zero was trained from a pretrained base model by reinforcement learning alone, with rewards for a correct final answer on mathematics and code and for the format of the output, using GRPO: PPO's clipped objective with the critic replaced by the mean and standard deviation of the rewards of a group of answers to the same prompt (Shao et al. 2024). Its accuracy on the AIME 2024 problems rose from 15.6% to 71.0% during training, and its answers grew longer, with checks and corrections of earlier steps that nobody had demonstrated. DeepSeek-R1 adds a small supervised start and further stages for readability and general use.
:::

:::thm Empirical | AlphaGeometry and AlphaCode
AlphaGeometry (Trinh et al. 2024) pairs a language model that proposes auxiliary constructions with a symbolic deduction engine that proves what follows from them; the model was trained on about 100 million synthetic theorems and proofs, with no human demonstrations, and solved 25 of 30 olympiad geometry problems in its benchmark. AlphaGeometry2 (Chervonyi et al. 2025) reports gold-medal level on olympiad geometry. AlphaCode (Li et al. 2022) samples very many candidate programs, filters them by the examples in the problem statement, clusters the rest and submits a few, reaching about the median of human competitors in Codeforces contests. All three put a verifier or a search next to the network: the network proposes, something exact checks.
:::

:::thm Contested | What reinforcement learning adds to a pretrained model
One view holds that RL with verifiable rewards teaches new reasoning; another, that it mainly makes the model choose, more reliably, solutions it could already produce with some probability, since sampling the base model many times often finds them too (Yue et al. 2025). Chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency, sampling several reasoning paths and taking the majority answer (Wei et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023), get part of the effect with no training at all, the theme of Denny Zhou's talk in the course.
:::

## Check yourself

:::check
A one-step problem: the action $a \in \{0, 1\}$ is drawn with $\pi_\theta(1) = \sigma(\theta)$, and the reward is $1$ for $a = 1$ and $0$ otherwise. Compute $\nabla_\theta J$ directly and from the policy gradient theorem. What is the variance of the single-sample estimate without a baseline and with $b = \sigma(\theta)$?
---
$J = \sigma(\theta)$, so $J' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$. The score is $\partial_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a) = a - \sigma$, so the estimate is $R\,(a - \sigma)$: it equals $1 - \sigma$ with probability $\sigma$ and $0$ otherwise, with mean $\sigma(1 - \sigma)$ and variance $\sigma(1 - \sigma)^2 - \sigma^2(1 - \sigma)^2 = \sigma(1 - \sigma)^3$. With $b = \sigma$ the estimate is $(a - \sigma)^2$, equal to $(1 - \sigma)^2$ with probability $\sigma$ and $\sigma^2$ otherwise; its variance is $\sigma(1 - \sigma)(1 - 2\sigma)^2$, smaller, and $0$ at $\sigma = \frac12$.
:::

:::check
For $A > 0$, write the gradient of $\min(rA, \mathrm{clip}(r, 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)A)$ in $r$ as a function of $r$, and the same for $A < 0$.
---
For $A > 0$: the gradient is $A$ for $r < 1 + \varepsilon$ and $0$ for $r > 1 + \varepsilon$; below $1 - \varepsilon$ it is still $A$, because the min picks $rA$ there. For $A < 0$: it is $0$ for $r < 1 - \varepsilon$ and $A$ for $r > 1 - \varepsilon$, including above $1 + \varepsilon$. In both cases the gradient vanishes only where the policy has already moved in the direction the sample asks for.
:::

:::check
Why does the performance difference lemma not by itself give an algorithm, and what does the surrogate $L_\pi$ change?
---
The lemma averages the advantages of $\pi$ over the states of the new policy $\pi'$, which are unknown before $\pi'$ is run, so it cannot be used to choose $\pi'$. The surrogate uses the old policy's states, which the batch provides; it matches the lemma to first order and is reliable only while $\pi'$ stays close to $\pi$, which is why TRPO constrains the KL divergence and PPO clips the ratio.
:::
