:::lede
A transformer layer lets every position of a sequence read from every other position, by a rule that is itself learned. The rule is one formula, and the formula is a softened version of looking something up in a table. Start with the lookup.
:::

:::widget softlookup

:::four
**What is it trying to do?** Map a sequence of vectors $(x_1, \dots, x_T)$ to a sequence $(y_1, \dots, y_T)$ in which each $y_i$ may depend on every $x_j$, with the dependence decided by content and not by distance, for any length $T$.
**Why can it be done this way?** A soft lookup is differentiable in the query, the keys and the values, so "where to read from" can be trained by gradient descent like any other parameter. The layer treats its input as a set; order is supplied separately. That separates what a token is from where it is.
**Why should it be done this way?** In a recurrent network (week 6) information from $j$ steps back passes through $j$ nonlinear maps, and the computation cannot be parallelized over positions. In attention every pair of positions is one step apart and all positions are processed at once, for a cost quadratic in $T$. On translation first, then on nearly everything, this traded well: an empirical outcome (Vaswani et al. 2017 and what followed).
**What comes out?** The layer behind nearly every current large language model (week 8), BERT and vision transformers. Here: a numerical check of the layer's symmetries, and a character model with 0.8 million parameters that trains in two to four minutes on a MacBook Air and ends far below the two counting baselines computed beside it: the table of character frequencies and the table of pair frequencies.
:::

## 1. Attention as a soft lookup

A dictionary returns the value whose key equals the query. Attention returns a weighted average of all the values, weighted by how well each key matches the query.

:::thm Definition | Scaled dot-product attention
Given queries $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{T'\times d_k}$, keys $K \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times d_k}$ and values $V \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_v}$, with one row per position,
$$\mathrm{Att}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\Bigl(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Bigr)\, V \ \in \mathbb{R}^{T'\times d_v},$$
where softmax is applied to each row. Row $i$ of $A = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})$ is a probability vector over the $T$ positions, the *attention weights* of query $i$, and row $i$ of the output is the corresponding average of the rows of $V$.
:::

Multiply all scores by a large constant and each row of $A$ concentrates on the best-matching key: an exact lookup. Multiply by a small one and every row becomes uniform: a plain average. The figure above moved between the two. The factor $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ sets where on that scale a freshly initialized layer starts.

:::thm Proposition | Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$
If the entries of $q, k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, then $q\cdot k$ has mean 0 and variance $d_k$.
:::proof Proof
$\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\sum_i q_ik_i\bigr) = \sum_i \mathbb{E}[q_i^2]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i^2] = d_k$, the cross terms having mean zero.
:::

So unscaled scores have typical size $\sqrt{d_k}$, and the softmax of numbers that large is nearly one-hot. A saturated softmax also has almost no gradient (its Jacobian is $\operatorname{diag}(p) - p^\top p$, which vanishes at a vertex of the simplex), so a layer that starts saturated barely trains. The first experiment measures the effect: with 32 random keys and $d_k = 256$, the largest attention weight averages 0.91 without the scaling and 0.16 with it.

## 2. Self-attention and its symmetries

In *self*-attention the queries, keys and values are all linear images of the same sequence $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times d}$:

$$\begin{gathered} \mathrm{SA}(X) = \mathrm{softmax}\Bigl(\frac{X\, W_Q W_K^\top X^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Bigr)\, X W_V, \\ W_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k},\quad W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_v}. \end{gathered}$$

The score between positions $i$ and $j$ is the bilinear form $x_i\, B\, x_j^\top$ with $B = W_QW_K^\top$. Only $B$ matters, and it has rank at most $d_k$: the two matrices are a low-rank factorization, and $(W_QM,\ W_KM^{-\top})$ gives the same layer for any invertible $M$.

:::thm Proposition | Permutation equivariance
For every permutation matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times T}$, $\ \mathrm{SA}(PX) = P\,\mathrm{SA}(X)$. The same holds for any composition of self-attention with maps applied to each position separately and with residual connections.
:::proof Proof
Let $S = XBX^\top/\sqrt{d_k}$. Then $(PX)B(PX)^\top/\sqrt{d_k} = PSP^\top$, and row-wise softmax satisfies $\mathrm{softmax}(PSP^\top) = P\,\mathrm{softmax}(S)\,P^\top$, because it commutes with permuting rows and with permuting the entries within every row. Hence $\mathrm{SA}(PX) = P\,\mathrm{softmax}(S)\,P^\top P\,XW_V = P\,\mathrm{SA}(X)$.
:::

So without further information the layer sees a bag of tokens: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" get the same outputs, attached to the same words. Order has to be put in by hand (section 4).

**The causal mask.** A language model predicts $x_{i+1}$ from $x_1, \dots, x_i$. To let one forward pass produce a legitimate prediction at every position, set the score $S_{ij}$ to $-\infty$ for $j > i$ before the softmax. Then $A$ is lower triangular, $y_i$ depends only on $x_1, \dots, x_i$, and a sequence of length $T$ yields $T$ training examples at the cost of one pass. (The lecture notes speak of upper-triangularity: the same condition for the transposed matrix, with rows indexed by keys and columns by queries.)

:::thm Proposition | What the mask does to the symmetry
With the causal mask, $y_i$ is a function of $x_i$ and of the multiset $\{x_j : j \le i\}$. It is unchanged by any permutation of the earlier positions $1, \dots, i-1$, and the layer as a whole is no longer permutation-equivariant.
:::proof Proof
$y_i = \sum_{j\le i} \mathrm{softmax}_j\bigl(x_iBx_j^\top/\sqrt{d_k}\bigr)\, x_jW_V$ is a symmetric function of $(x_j)_{j\le i}$ for fixed $x_i$. Equivariance fails because a permutation changes which positions lie before $i$.
:::

:::fig mask | The attention weights of a causal layer for T = 6. Row i holds the weights of output i. The mask empties everything to the right of the diagonal, so output i averages inputs 1, …, i, and each row can be trained to predict the next token: six examples from one pass.

The first experiment checks all three statements to rounding error: the Jacobian $\partial y_i/\partial x_j$ vanishes for $j > i$; the unmasked layer is equivariant; the masked one is not, but its last output ignores a shuffle of everything before it.

**Several heads.** A single softmax row is one distribution over positions, so one head reads from essentially one place at a time. Multi-head attention runs $h$ heads with separate $W_Q^a, W_K^a, W_V^a$ of width $d_k = d_v = d/h$, concatenates their outputs, and applies one more matrix $W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. The parameter count is $3\cdot h\cdot d\cdot\frac dh + d^2 = 4d^2$, the same as one head of full width, and the model can look at $h$ places at once.

## 3. The transformer block

A block combines attention, which moves information between positions, with a small network applied to each position separately, which processes it. In the pre-normalization form used by GPT-2 and by the experiment below,

$$\begin{gathered} u = x + \mathrm{MHA}\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(x)\bigr), \quad y = u + \mathrm{MLP}\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(u)\bigr), \\ \mathrm{MLP}(v) = \mathrm{GELU}(vW_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2, \end{gathered}$$

with $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times 4d}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{4d\times d}$, the same weights at every position, and LN the layer normalization of week 4.

:::fig block | One block, drawn for one position. The vertical line is the residual stream: each sublayer reads a normalized copy and adds its output back. Attention is the only arrow that crosses between positions.

A block has $4d^2$ parameters in the attention and $8d^2$ in the MLP, about $12d^2$ in all. Because each sublayer adds to its input, the identity is always available and the gradient of the loss reaches every block directly, as in residual networks (week 5). It is common to describe the running sum as a *residual stream* that sublayers read from and write to (Elhage et al. 2021); this is a way of speaking, useful for interpretability work, not a theorem.

:::thm Empirical | Where the normalization goes
The original transformer normalizes after the residual addition, $y = \mathrm{LN}(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x))$, and needs a learning-rate warm-up to train. Normalizing the input of each sublayer instead leaves the residual path untouched and trains stably with little or no warm-up (Xiong et al. 2020). Most current models use the second form.
:::

## 4. Position

Since the block is permutation-equivariant, position is added to the input: $\tilde x_i = x_i + r(i)$. The original choice is a fixed set of sinusoids, for $k = 0, \dots, d/2 - 1$ and $\omega_k = 10000^{-2k/d}$:

$$r(i)_{2k} = \sin(\omega_k i), \qquad r(i)_{2k+1} = \cos(\omega_k i).$$

:::thm Proposition | Shifts act by rotations
For every offset $m$ there is an orthogonal matrix $R_m$, block diagonal with $2\times2$ rotations by the angles $\omega_k m$, such that $r(i + m) = r(i)\,R_m$ for all $i$. Consequently $\langle r(i), r(j)\rangle = \sum_k \cos\bigl(\omega_k(i - j)\bigr)$ depends only on $i - j$.
:::proof Proof
The addition formulas for sine and cosine, applied to each pair $(\sin\omega_ki, \cos\omega_ki)$.
:::

So "the position $m$ steps back" is a fixed linear function of the encoding, which a query matrix can represent. The wavelengths run geometrically from $2\pi$ to $10000\cdot2\pi$.

Three alternatives are in use. A *learned* table $R \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\max}\times d}$ with $r(i)$ its $i$-th row (BERT, GPT-2, the experiment below). *Relative* encodings, where the score between $i$ and $j$ gets a term depending on $i - j$ (Transformer-XL, one of this week's case studies). And *rotary* encodings (Su et al. 2021), which rotate queries and keys by position-dependent angles, so that scores depend on $i - j$ by the proposition above; many recent language models use these.

:::thm Empirical | A causal model can find position by itself (Haviv, Ram, Press, Izsak, Levy 2022)
Causal language models trained with no positional encoding at all remain competitive with standard ones, and their hidden states can be probed for absolute position. The causal mask makes this possible: it breaks the permutation symmetry (section 2), and for instance uniform attention over the first $i$ positions puts weight $1/i$ on each, which reveals $i$.
:::

The second experiment has a switch for this. In the reference runs on a MacBook Air M5 the validation loss went from 1.58 to 1.66 nats per character without the position table, and from 1.58 to 1.64 with another seed: worse, and still far below the 2.48 of the pair-frequency table. Two pairs of runs fix the direction; do not read much into the second decimal.

## 5. Training a language model

Fix a vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ (here the 65 characters that occur in the text) and view a text as a sequence $c_1, \dots, c_N$. The model outputs, at each position, logits over $\mathcal V$ for the next token, and the loss is the average cross-entropy

$$L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{t\in I} \ln p_\theta\bigl(c_{t+1} \mid c_{t-T+1}, \dots, c_t\bigr),$$

in nats per character; dividing by $\ln 2$ gives bits. By the chain rule of probability the sum is $-\ln p_\theta(\text{text})$, so this is maximum likelihood (week 3), and the loss in bits is the length per character at which an arithmetic coder driven by the model would compress the text (week 2). To generate, sample $c_{t+1}$ from $\mathrm{softmax}(z/\tau)$ with a temperature $\tau$, append it, and repeat.

A loss is only informative next to baselines. On the held-out last tenth of Tiny Shakespeare (real text, 1,115,394 characters):

| model | validation loss, nats per character |
|---|---|
| uniform over 65 characters | $\ln 65 = 4.17$ |
| character frequencies | 3.35 |
| table of next-character frequencies given the previous character | 2.48 |
| the experiment's transformer after 2,000 steps (reference run) | 1.58 |

The first three are computed inside the experiment from counts; the last is one training run on a MacBook Air M5, which took 102 seconds. The same run with two other seeds ended at 1.58 as well, and took four minutes each on the same machine in another session; why it was slower then was not tracked down. Expect two to four minutes.

## 6. Cost, and the comparison with recurrence and convolution

For sequence length $n$, width $d$ and kernel size $k$ (Vaswani et al. 2017, Table 1):

| layer | work per layer | sequential steps | longest path between positions |
|---|---|---|---|
| self-attention | $O(n^2 d)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| recurrent | $O(n d^2)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| convolutional | $O(k n d^2)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(\log_k n)$ |

Attention is cheaper than recurrence while $n < d$, and its quadratic growth in $n$ is what limits context length.

The original transformer was an encoder–decoder for translation: six blocks on each side, unmasked self-attention in the encoder, masked self-attention plus *cross*-attention in the decoder (queries from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder's output). The base model has $d = 512$, 8 heads, MLP width 2048 and 65 million parameters; the big one $d = 1024$, 16 heads, MLP width 4096 and 213 million parameters, trained for 3.5 days on eight GPUs to 28.4 BLEU on English–German and 41.8 on English–French (WMT 2014).

## 7. Three descendants

These are the week's case-study papers; the Sources tab links them.

**BERT** (Devlin et al. 2019) keeps only the encoder, so every position sees both directions. It is pretrained to fill in masked tokens: 15% of positions are selected, and of those 80% are replaced by a mask symbol, 10% by a random token and 10% left unchanged. A second task predicts whether one sentence follows another. The pretrained model is then fine-tuned for each task. BERT-Base has 12 blocks, $d = 768$, 12 heads, 110 million parameters; BERT-Large 24 blocks, $d = 1024$, 16 heads, 340 million.

**Vision transformer** (Dosovitskiy et al. 2021). Cut an image into $16\times16$ patches, flatten each (768 numbers for an RGB patch), embed linearly, add learned position embeddings and a class token, and apply a standard encoder. With less built-in structure than a convolutional network (week 5) it does worse than comparable residual networks when trained on ImageNet alone and better when pretrained on much larger collections. ViT-Base has 12 blocks, $d = 768$, 86 million parameters; ViT-Large 24 blocks, $d = 1024$, 307 million; ViT-Huge 32 blocks, $d = 1280$, 632 million.

**Transformer-XL** (Dai et al. 2019) extends the context of a language model beyond one segment by caching the hidden states of the previous segment and attending to them, and introduces relative positional encodings so that cached states remain meaningful when the window moves.

:::check
A causal model has no positional encoding. Can a one-layer model's output at position 3 tell the input "abc" from "bac"? Can a two-layer model's?
---
One layer: no. By the proposition on the mask, $y_3$ depends on $x_3 = \mathrm{c}$ and on the multiset $\{\mathrm a, \mathrm b, \mathrm c\}$, which are the same for both inputs. Two layers: yes. After the first layer, position 2 holds a function of ($\mathrm b$, having seen $\{\mathrm a, \mathrm b\}$) in one case and of ($\mathrm a$, having seen $\{\mathrm a, \mathrm b\}$) in the other, and position 1 differs as well; the second layer at position 3 receives different multisets.
:::
